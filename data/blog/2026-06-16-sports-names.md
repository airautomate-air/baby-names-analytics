Athletes affect baby names — but not in the ways you'd expect. The greatest player in the NBA's history had a massive naming effect. One of the greatest players today has essentially none. A soccer star who moved to LA transformed an obscure surname into a mainstream first name. And a superstar's death produced a naming spike unlike almost anything else in the data.

Here's the full picture from SSA data.

---

## Jordan — the gold standard (1990s)

**[Jordan](/name/jordan.html)** was #29 for boys in 1990. That year, Michael Jordan was 26 years old, in his fifth NBA season, and becoming the most recognizable athlete in the world. The name had already climbed — Jordan was #60 for boys in 1985 — but the Jordan era transformed it.

| Year | Boys Rank | Girls Rank | Context |
|------|-----------|-----------|---------|
| 1988 | ~#50 | ~#90 | Jordan's first MVP |
| 1990 | #29 | #59 | First championship run |
| 1992 | #32 | #58 | Dream Team, Olympics |
| 1993 | #28 | #58 | Third championship |
| 1995 | #29 | — | Jordan returns from baseball |
| 1997 | #26 | — | Fifth/sixth championships |
| 2000 | #36 | #50 | Post-Jordan |
| 2010 | #46 | #178 | Decline begins |
| 2024 | **#104** | **#539** | Still present but falling |

Jordan's naming effect is the largest and most sustained athlete-naming effect in SSA data:
- 16,000+ babies named Jordan per year through the 1990s (both sexes)
- Broke through as a girls' name simultaneously — a rare feat for a boys' name
- The effect outlasted Jordan's career by years (still #26 in 1997, two full years after his second retirement)

The mechanism is well-understood: Jordan was everywhere — on Air Jordan sneakers, on cereal boxes, in *Space Jam*, on every basketball court in America. He was an aspirational figure for a generation. Parents who loved Jordan named their sons after him; the name crossed to girls because the cultural moment was large enough to carry both.

## Kobe — the death spike

**[Kobe](/name/kobe.html)** has two distinct naming stories: the NBA-debut spike (1996–2000) and the death spike (2020).

Kobe Bryant entered the NBA in 1996 at age 17 — the youngest player ever drafted at that time. Kobe went from essentially unused (#1,271 in 1996) to #238 in 2000, with 1,432 babies. He was the most visible young player in the league.

Then the name fell — #544 in 2005, hovering around #400–600 for a decade.

On January 26, 2020, Kobe Bryant died in a helicopter crash in Calabasas, California, along with his 13-year-old daughter Gianna and seven others. That year, Kobe doubled in use:

| Year | Rank | Count |
|------|------|-------|
| 2019 | #557 | 506 |
| **2020** | **#239** | **1,515** |
| 2021 | #298 | 1,116 |
| 2022 | #381 | 851 |
| 2023 | #418 | 755 |
| 2024 | #409 | 787 |

From 506 babies to 1,515 in a single year — a near-tripling. This is the clearest death-tribute naming spike in recent SSA data. Parents who had loved Kobe Bryant and felt his death deeply named their sons after him in 2020.

The spike has partly faded but held significantly above pre-death levels — #409 in 2024 vs. #557 in 2019. Kobe's legacy as a tribute name has lasting power.

## Beckham — the transformation

**[Beckham](/name/beckham.html)** (#168, 2,180 babies) is the most remarkable athlete-naming story currently unfolding. Unlike Jordan and Kobe — which were first names that *also* happened to be worn by great athletes — Beckham was a pure surname, essentially unused as a given name, that transformed into a mainstream first name.

David Beckham (born 1975) rose to global fame with Manchester United and England in the late 1990s. But the American naming effect only accelerated after he moved to MLS's LA Galaxy in 2007:

| Year | Beckham Rank | Count |
|------|-------------|-------|
| 2003 | #2,790 | 35 |
| 2007 | #1,074 | 173 |
| 2010 | #749 | 303 |
| 2015 | #359 | 923 |
| 2020 | #248 | 1,411 |
| 2024 | **#168** | **2,180** |

35 babies in 2003. 2,180 in 2024. Beckham is now more popular as a first name than Brooklyn (#170, which is also a Beckham son's name). This is genuine cultural adoption — Beckham has become a first name, not a tribute to David Beckham specifically.

The pattern here is different from Jordan or Kobe: Beckham works because:
1. It's a surname that sounds like modern American names (rhymes with Beckett, Breckin, Mackem)
2. Parents associate it with success and style, not a specific person
3. The Beckham brand extends to fashion and family — not just sport

## Brady — the Super Bowl correlation

**[Brady](/name/brady.html)** (#309, 1,098 babies) shows a clear correlation with Tom Brady's Super Bowl wins. Brady was a common Irish-American surname, used as a first name since the 1990s (The Brady Bunch predated Tom Brady's career), but the NFL-era usage is distinct.

Tom Brady won his first Super Bowl in February 2002:

| Year | Brady Rank | Context |
|------|-----------|---------|
| 2000 | #189 | Pre-Brady |
| 2002 | #147 | First Super Bowl win |
| 2003 | #142 | Second win |
| 2005 | **#110** | Third win, MVP |
| 2010 | #127 | Post-peak |
| 2020 | #304 | Declining |
| 2024 | #309 | Stable |

The correlation is real — Brady peaked during Brady's championship run — but the effect is modest compared to Jordan. Tom Brady won seven Super Bowls; the naming effect shows a clear climb followed by a plateau.

Brady's limitation: it was already a name before Tom Brady, so parents couldn't use it as an unambiguous tribute.

## LeBron — zero babies

LeBron James won four NBA championships, four MVP awards, and is the NBA's all-time leading scorer. He is arguably the most dominant basketball player since Michael Jordan.

In 2024, there were essentially zero babies named LeBron in the SSA data.

The difference from Jordan is structural: *LeBron* is a unique invented name, created by his mother (likely a variant of the French *le bron*, "the brown"). It reads as one person's name — specifically *that* person. Jordan could be a given name or a place name or a biblical name; anyone could have a Jordan in the family. LeBron can only be LeBron James.

This is the fundamental rule of athlete-naming: surnames and multi-purpose first names generate naming effects; unique-to-one-person names do not.

## Serena — the decline paradox

**[Serena](/name/serena.html)** (#332, 938 babies). Serena Williams won her first US Open title in 1999 and went on to win 23 Grand Slam titles — the most in the Open Era. In 2024, Serena is at #332.

In 1999, the year Serena Williams first emerged, Serena was #218 with 1,440 babies. By 2010, it had fallen to #449. It has partially recovered to #332 in 2024 — but Serena has never surpassed its pre-Serena-Williams level.

The mechanism: Serena was already a declining mid-century name when Williams became famous. The Williams association provided a floor — the name never fell off the chart entirely — but couldn't reverse the overall trend. The same pattern applies to many celebrity names: fame *slows* a falling name more than it *propels* a new one.

## Ronaldo and Messi — the soccer exception

Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi are the two most famous soccer players in history, both widely considered the greatest players of their era.

- **Ronaldo**: #889 in 2000, **#1,946 in 2024** (declining throughout Ronaldo's career)
- **Messi**: ~6 babies per year in 2024, statistically negligible

Soccer's American naming effect is essentially zero, despite the sport's growth in the US. The reason: the core American sport culture (NFL, NBA, MLB) still drives naming in ways soccer doesn't, and Ronaldo/Messi are perceived as international names rather than aspirational American names.

## The Laila Ali story

**[Laila](/name/laila.html)** — #371 (839 babies). Laila Ali, Muhammad Ali's daughter, became a professional boxer in 1999. Laila was #1,164 in 1999 and climbed sharply:

| Year | Rank |
|------|------|
| 1999 | #1,164 |
| 2000 | #644 |
| 2001 | #453 |
| 2005 | #234 |
| 2010 | **#140** |

But Laila is also an Arabic/Swahili name meaning "night" that was climbing in Muslim-American and African-American communities independently. The Laila Ali effect is real — the timing is clear — but the two trends are intertwined.

## Beckham vs. Brooklyn — the celeb-kid names

David and Victoria Beckham named their four children Brooklyn, Romeo, Cruz, and Harper. All four have had naming effects:

- **Brooklyn** — girls #170: steadily climbing as a place name
- **Romeo** — boys: still rare, but visible
- **Harper** — girls #23: massive rise, though Taylor Swift's father's name is Harper, and the "strong surname" trend is larger than any one celebrity
- **Cruz** — boys: modest use

The Beckham children's names show how celebrity baby naming can introduce surnames into the naming pool. Harper's rise to #23 is the most dramatic — though attributing it entirely to the Beckhams would be an overstatement given how many drivers the name has.

## The rules of athlete naming

From the data, the patterns are clear:

**What drives athlete-naming:**
- Extended dominance over a decade (Jordan), not a single championship
- Surnames that sound like modern first names (Beckham, Brady)
- Death tributes for beloved athletes who died suddenly (Kobe)
- Athletes who are aspirational across culture, not just in their sport

**What doesn't work:**
- Unique names tied to one specific person (LeBron, Messi)
- Athletes who are famous in sports Americans don't prioritize (Ronaldo, Messi)
- Names already in decline that can't be reversed by fame alone (Serena)

The Jordan effect — 16,000+ babies per year for a decade — remains the ceiling for what an athlete can do to American naming. No athlete since has come close.
