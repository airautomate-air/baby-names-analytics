## Which Slavic traditions?

"Slavic" covers an enormous linguistic and cultural territory: Russian, Polish, Ukrainian, Czech, Slovak, Serbo-Croatian, Bulgarian, Slovenian. Each tradition has its own name stock, though with significant overlap. The US chart draws from several:

**Russian names** dominate in volume, reflecting the size of the Russian-American population and Russian culture's global reach through literature, music, and now streaming media. Ivan, Nikolai, Anastasia, Natasha, Sasha, and Tatiana are all primarily Russian in US cultural coding.

**Polish names** are also well-represented, concentrated in cities with large Polish-American populations: Chicago, Detroit, Buffalo, Milwaukee. Adrian, Aleksander, Filip, and Karina are often Polish in US usage.

**Ukrainian names** have been growing since 2022. Bohdan (a traditional Ukrainian boy's name meaning "given by God"), Sofiia (the Ukrainian spelling of Sofia), and Daryna are appearing in US data that showed none of these names before. The numbers remain small but the direction is clear.

**Czech and Slovak** names contribute a smaller stream — Vaclav, Lubomir, Marek — mostly through the Czech-American community in Texas, Nebraska, and the Midwest.

## How Slavic endings work

Slavic naming has a distinctive morphological structure that makes many names feel immediately recognizable:

**-ov / -ev** (masculine) and **-ova / -eva** (feminine) are the most common surname suffixes in Russian, Czech, and other Slavic languages. They're not typically used in given names but appear in the surnames that occasionally cross into first-name use.

**-mir** (from *mir*, "peace" or "world") appears in many compound Slavic names: Vladimir ("rules + world"), Casimir (Polish, "proclaiming peace"), Dragomir, Ljubomir. Few of these compounds have crossed to the US, but Casimir (#1,834) is present.

**-slav** (from *slava*, "glory") forms another set of compound names: Vladislav, Stanislav, Miroslav, Bronislaw. In the US these appear almost entirely within the Polish-American community and are rare in general data.

**-a endings for girls** — Mila, Natalia, Anastasia, Vera, Lena — fit the modern US preference for names ending in -a, which is why Slavic girls' names have crossed more successfully than boys' names.

**Diminutive forms** are a major feature of Slavic naming culture. Russians in particular use diminutives affectionately — Natasha for Natalia, Sasha for Alexandra, Anya for Anna, Masha for Maria. These diminutive forms have often crossed to the US as standalone first names.

## The US adoption curve

The pattern for Slavic names arriving in the US follows a consistent three-stage arc:

**Stage 1 (Early 1900s):** Immigration wave. Polish, Russian, Ukrainian, and Czech immigrants arrive, often with given names from home. Children frequently receive Anglicised names (Jan → John, Aleksander → Alexander) to assimilate.

**Stage 2 (Post-WWII):** Suppression. During the Cold War, Eastern European names carried an awkward political valence. Many families Anglicised further. Ivan and Natasha had a specific problem: *Ivan the Terrible* and Cold War spy stereotypes made them feel loaded.

**Stage 3 (1990s–present):** Cultural revival. As the Cold War recedes and global media normalizes Slavic names, they re-enter the chart. Mila Kunis normalised Mila as a standalone name. *Bridgerton*, *Anya Taylor-Joy*, and *The Witcher* have done similar work for Anya and Anastasia.

## What's driving it

Three forces, in roughly increasing order of recent impact:

1. **The Polish-American and Russian-American populations** of the Midwest and Northeast have always supplied a steady trickle of Slavic names. This is the historical base.
2. **Ukrainian immigration since 2022** is changing the mix. Names like **Bohdan**, **Sofiia**, **Olha**, **Yaroslav**, and **Mariia** are appearing in US data that didn't before. The numbers are small but growing.
3. **Romance-novel and HBO normalisation.** *Bridgerton*'s Anthony, *The Witcher*'s Geralt and Yennefer (Slavic-flavoured fantasy), *Anya Taylor-Joy*, *Yelena Belova* from Marvel — Slavic-coded names have gone from rare to routine in popular media. **Mila Kunis** did the single-name job for Mila a decade earlier.

## Frequently asked questions

**What's the difference between Mila, Milla, and Milah?**
All three spellings appear in US data and are counted separately by the SSA. Mila (no double-l, no h) is the most common and most authentically Slavic spelling. The doubled-l variant (Milla) appears in some Germanic traditions. Milah is less common. The combined count across all spellings would push the name even higher.

**Why is Ivan (#153) so much lower than John (#21) given that they're the same name?**
Naming perception is cultural, not etymological. Most American parents choosing Ivan are doing so because it sounds Russian and interesting — not because they know it's the same name as John. Ivan sat under the long Cold War shadow and is only now recovering. At #153 it's climbing; it was well below #200 as recently as a decade ago.

**Are any Slavic names in the top 20?**
Mila (#33) is the closest. If you include names with disputed or multiple origins, Vera (#226) and Nina (#321) could also be argued. The reason no Slavic names are in the very top tier is partly historical (Cold War stigma) and partly phonological — Russian names often have consonant clusters (Vladimir, Vladislav) that don't sit easily in English.

**What Slavic name has the best chance of breaking into the top 50?**
Lena (#263) and Anya (#394) both have the modern profile — short, vowel-rich, two-syllable, easy to spell. Either could land in the top 100 within five years. Vera is already at #226 and climbing, helped by the vintage revival (it's also a 1920s name making a comeback).
