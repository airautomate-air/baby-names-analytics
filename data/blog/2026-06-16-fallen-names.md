Every generation produces a set of names that feel universal at the time and dated within a decade. Jennifer, Crystal, Tiffany, and Heather are the classic examples for the late 20th century — names that were given to hundreds of thousands of children at their peak and that now feel almost exclusively associated with a specific era.

Here's a data-driven look at the steepest falls in American naming history.

---

## The biggest fall: Jennifer

**[Jennifer](/name/jennifer.html)** — peak 1972: 63,602 babies (#1). 2024: #546 (552 babies).

Jennifer is the most dramatic single name story in the SSA dataset. It went from #1 for fourteen consecutive years (1970–1983) to #546 in 2024. At its peak, more than 1 in every 33 girls born in the United States was named Jennifer. In 2024, only 552 girls were named Jennifer — a 99.1% reduction.

The mechanism is well-established in naming research: names that reach extreme popularity within a single generation become permanently associated with that generation. A Jennifer born in 1975 is now 50 years old. New parents in 2024 are typically in their late 20s or early 30s — the same generation as Jennifers. Naming your daughter Jennifer in 2024 would feel like naming her after your college roommates.

Jennifer's fall is particularly steep because the peak was so high. The 63,602 babies in 1972 is the single largest one-year count for any girls' name in the post-1950s era.

---

## Jessica — 55,996 babies in 1987, now #574

**[Jessica](/name/jessica.html)** — peak 1987: 55,996 babies. 2024: #574 (526 babies).

Jessica followed Jennifer at #1 from 1985 to 1995. At its peak (1987), more than 55,000 girls were named Jessica. Now it's at #574 with 526 babies — a 99% drop. The same generational mechanism applies: Jessicas were born in the late 1980s and early 1990s; naming a child Jessica today would feel like naming her after a friend from high school.

William Shakespeare created the name Jessica for *The Merchant of Venice* (1596) — he apparently invented it from a Hebrew name mentioned in the Old Testament. It went virtually unused until the 1940s, rose through the 1970s, and peaked in the late 1980s. Its arc is almost perfectly symmetric: 400 years of obscurity, 40 years of rise, a 15-year peak, and now a rapid fall.

---

## Ashley — 54,853 babies in 1987, now #124

**[Ashley](/name/ashley.html)** — peak 1987: 54,853 babies. 2024: #124 (2,231 babies).

Ashley is the counterexample to Jennifer and Jessica. It also peaked at #1 in the late 1980s — but it's held at #124 in 2024, far better than its female contemporaries. The difference may be that Ashley started as a gender-neutral name (it was originally predominantly male, as a surname), which has given it more flexibility. It's also still declining; the question is whether it plateaus or continues downward.

---

## Heather — 24,302 in 1975, now #1,356

**[Heather](/name/heather.html)** — peak 1975: 24,302 babies. 2024: #1,356 (168 babies).

Heather peaked earlier than Jennifer and Jessica and has fallen further in relative terms. The *Heathers* film (1988) is now more associated with the name than any actual Heather — the black comedy about high school social hierarchy has kept the name's era association ironically present.

---

## Crystal — 19,098 in 1982, now #1,173

**[Crystal](/name/crystal.html)** — peak 1982: 19,098 babies. 2024: #1,173 (203 babies).

Crystal is the *Dynasty* name. Joan Collins played the villainous Alexis Carrington (not Crystal), but Linda Evans played Crystal Carrington in the show that defined 1980s prime-time soap opera. Crystal's spike in 1981–1985 tracks almost perfectly to the show's peak viewership. It never recovered after *Dynasty* ended in 1989. See [pop culture names](/blog/pop-culture-names.html).

---

## Tiffany — 18,363 in 1988, now #840

**[Tiffany](/name/tiffany.html)** — peak 1988: 18,363 babies. 2024: #840 (320 babies).

Tiffany peaked in 1988 — driven by the luxury jeweler's name and the general 1980s taste for aspirational brand associations. Tiffany Darwish ("Tiffany"), the pop singer, was a top artist in 1987–1988 and may have contributed. Now at #840, still declining.

---

## Brittany — 37,791 in 1989, now #791

**[Brittany](/name/brittany.html)** — peak 1989: 37,791 babies. 2024: #791 (355 babies).

Brittany (and its variant Britney) peaked around 1989–1992. Britney Spears (born 1981) was not responsible for the name's peak — she was herself named at the height of the Brittany era. The name now exists primarily as a Britney Spears reference, which does not appear to be generating naming momentum.

## The boys' side of the same story

### Gary — 38,753 in 1952, now #1,129

**[Gary](/name/gary.html)** — peak 1952: 38,753 babies. 2024: #1,129 (187 babies).

Gary is the paradigm case for mid-century American naming. It peaked in 1952 and has been declining ever since. It was never as pop-culture specific as Jennifer or Crystal — its fall reflects more the general mid-century name cycle (alongside Larry, Barry, and Jerry) than a specific cultural event. These are the names that feel "Boomer" in a way that no specific film or TV show created.

### Jason — 55,636 in 1977, now #148

**[Jason](/name/jason.html)** — peak 1977: 55,636 babies. 2024: #148 (2,448 babies).

Jason is Ashley's equivalent on the boys' side — a name that peaked extremely high and has held at a respectable mid-chart position rather than collapsing. #148 in 2024 is a significant fall from #1 in the late 1970s, but it's far from the floor that Chad (#1,808), Todd (#1,588), and Craig (#1,833) have reached.

The *Friday the 13th* Jason Voorhees (1980–present) may be a factor in the name's staying power: the villain has kept the name consistently present in pop culture, even if the association isn't exactly aspirational.

### Chad — 13,391 in 1972, now #1,808

**[Chad](/name/chad.html)** — peak 1972: 13,391 babies. 2024: #1,808 (90 babies).

Chad is the most dramatically stigmatized name in American culture since its 1970s peak. The "Chad" internet slang (meaning an extremely confident, stereotypically attractive man) has given the name a specific cultural connotation that makes it difficult to use without irony. It dropped to #1,808 in 2024.

### Todd — 15,349 in 1964, now #1,588

**[Todd](/name/todd.html)** — peak 1964: 15,349 babies. 2024: #1,588 (108 babies).

Todd, Craig, and Gary represent the mid-century boys' names that peaked in the 1950s–1970s and have no path back. They feel definitively like names for men in their 60s and 70s, with no vintage-revival energy attached.

## Why do names fall?

**Generational saturation.** When a name is given to 1 in every 30 girls in a single decade, it becomes permanently identified with women of that generation. New parents — themselves not named Jennifer — avoid the association.

**Novelty exhaustion.** Jennifer was new and interesting in 1965. By 1985, every elementary school had six of them. The name's ubiquity made it boring before it had time to cycle into nostalgia.

**Pop-culture ceiling.** Names driven by a specific show or moment (Crystal-Dynasty, Tiffany-brand) have a ceiling defined by that moment. Once the show ends, the name becomes dated.

**The vintage revival window.** Names need about 60–80 years before they can start to seem "fresh" again. Mary (peaked 1921) is just beginning to revive. Dorothy (peaked 1923) is considering it. Jennifer (peaked 1972) has probably 20–30 more years before its vintage moment arrives — if it ever does.
