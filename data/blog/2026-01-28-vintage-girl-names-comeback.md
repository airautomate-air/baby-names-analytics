There's a 100-year rule in baby naming: names that were popular in your great-grandmother's day are far enough removed to feel fresh again. The 1920s names are right in the sweet spot, and a handful of them have come all the way back.

To make this list, a name had to be:

- **Top 200** for US girls in the 1920s
- **Dropped well below 500** for at least one decade between 1970 and 2000
- Back in the **2024 top 250**

The result is a tight pack of 12 names — many of them now firmly in the modern top 100.

## The most dramatic recoveries

- **Violet** went from #77 in the 1920s, all the way down to #734 in its 1990 low, and is now the **#15 girl name in the US**. That's a swing of over 700 places back into the very top of the chart.
- **Hazel** dropped to #676 and is now **#19** — a similar reclamation. Both names benefit from the same aesthetic (short, vowel-rich, color-tied, vintage-coded).
- **Olive**'s low point was #3,677 — nearly out of the dataset entirely. It's back at #171 today, propelled in part by its proximity to chart-topper Olivia.

## Why these and not others?

Many 1920s names *didn't* come back. Gertrude, Mildred, Beatrice, and Doris all peaked in the same decade but have stayed out of the top 500 in 2024. The names that returned tend to share three properties:

1. **Short or visually compact**: Ada, Lila, Vera, Olive, Hazel.
2. **Soft endings** — "-ie", "-a", "-ose": Elsie, Stella, Rosalie, Eloise.
3. **Nature or virtue ties**: Violet, Olive, Hazel, Vera ("truth"), Stella ("star").

## Why vintage is winning right now

The vintage naming wave is not an accident or a whim — it reflects several converging forces:

**The 100-year distance.** A name that was popular in your great-grandmother's generation carries no living association with "old people." Vera and Hazel aren't names of nursing-home residents (those would be Linda, Sandra, Barbara); they're names that exist in a romantic, sepia-toned past rather than an adjacent generation.

**The over-saturation of recent names.** A generation of parents who were Emilys and Jennifers didn't want another Emily or Jennifer. They reached back past the 1980s–1990s glut to an era that felt genuinely different. The vintage names filled the demand for distinctiveness without requiring an invented name.

**Cultural reinforcement.** Downton Abbey, Call the Midwife, various 1920s-set films and shows have kept the aesthetic visible. When audiences fall in love with a character named Violet Crawley or Elsie Hughes, the names gain cultural warmth that persists into naming decisions.

**The nature-and-virtue connection.** Modern parents value names that carry meaning beyond their sound. Violet, Hazel, Olive (nature), Vera (truth), Stella (star), Eloise (various interpretations including "healthy") all have embedded significance that feels intentional.

## Decade comparison

The vintage wave is actually repeating a historical pattern:

- In the **1960s**, names popular in the 1870s–1880s (Victoria, Emily, Emma) began cautiously returning.
- In the **1990s**, names popular in the 1900s–1910s (Grace, Ruby, Lily) came back in force.
- In the **2020s**, names popular in the 1920s–1930s (Violet, Hazel, Mabel) are making their move.

If the pattern continues — and it shows no sign of stopping — the names popular in the **1940s** (Sandra, Barbara, Shirley, Donna) will be the next candidates for a comeback attempt around the 2040s. That seems impossible right now; it seemed impossible for Mabel in 2005 too.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did these names almost disappear in the first place?**
The mid-century (1940s–1970s) was an era of deliberate modernity — parents wanted names that felt fresh and contemporary, not inherited from grandparents. Names like Dorothy, Mabel, and Vera were associated with the Depression and the world before television. The generational desire to break with the past temporarily wiped out huge swaths of the Victorian and Edwardian name stock.

**Which of the 12 comeback names has the most room left to grow?**
Mabel (#222) and Ada (#193) both still have significant runway. Violet (#15) and Hazel (#19) are already near their likely ceiling given the fragmentation of the modern chart. Eloise (#64) is still climbing and could break the top 50.

**Is the vintage trend affecting boys' names too?**
Yes, though more slowly. Theodore (#4), Henry (#6), and Walter (still in the 300s but rising) are 1920s boys' names that have returned. The boys' vintage chart is generally about a decade behind the girls' side.

**Could Gertrude or Mildred ever come back?**
The statistical answer is: not within the next 20 years at their current trajectories. Both names fail the soft-ending and phonetic-profile tests. But a generation further on? Mildred's short form Millie is already top 200. The reintroduction sometimes starts from the nickname.
