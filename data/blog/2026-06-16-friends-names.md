*Friends* premiered on September 22, 1994, and ran for 10 seasons until May 2004. At its peak, 52 million Americans watched a single episode. It remains one of the most-streamed shows in the world.

Six characters. Six names. Thirty years of SSA data. What did the show actually do to the names — and why did some survive better than others?

---

## Rachel: the #13 name that couldn't hold on

**[Rachel](/name/rachel.html)** — Girls #246, 1,285 babies in 2024.

Rachel was already a popular name when *Friends* premiered. In 1994 it was #13 with 15,492 babies — a top-15 girls name driven by biblical heritage and a clean, feminine sound.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #13 | 15,492 |
| 2000 | #21 | 10,687 |
| 2005 | #38 | 6,223 |
| 2010 | #99 | 3,023 |
| 2015 | #165 | 1,917 |
| 2020 | #204 | 1,593 |
| 2024 | #246 | 1,285 |

Rachel's trajectory shows something important: the show didn't save the name. Rachel Green was one of the most famous TV characters of the era, but the name Rachel was already declining from a late-1980s/early-1990s peak. By the time the show was over, Rachel had fallen out of the top 50. By 2024 it's at #246.

This is the same pattern as Taylor Swift and the name Taylor (see [Taylor Swift names](/blog/taylor-swift-names.html)) — a famous name association doesn't reverse an ongoing decline if the decline is driven by deeper generational forces. Rachel sounds unmistakably like a late-1980s name to anyone who went to school in that era.

## Monica: the bigger fall

**[Monica](/name/monica.html)** — Girls #723, 388 babies in 2024.

Monica had the most dramatic decline of the Friends name set.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #88 | 3,912 |
| 2000 | #167 | 1,992 |
| 2005 | #238 | 1,409 |
| 2010 | #361 | 887 |
| 2015 | #505 | 576 |
| 2020 | #618 | 431 |
| 2024 | **#723** | **388** |

Monica in 2024 gets fewer babies than **Mia** (12,113) gets in a single month. The Monica Lewinsky scandal of 1998 — right in the middle of *Friends*' peak seasons — almost certainly accelerated the name's decline. Cultural associations are powerful, and a name that appears in both a sitcom and a political scandal simultaneously has competing pressures. Monica is now genuinely rare.

## Phoebe: the one that's actually rising

**[Phoebe](/name/phoebe.html)** — Girls #183, 1,639 babies in 2024.

Phoebe is the counterintuitive story in this dataset. When *Friends* premiered, Phoebe was obscure — #810 with just 248 babies in 1994. It was a Greek name (the Titaness of the moon), vaguely literary, not mainstream.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #810 | 248 |
| 2000 | #439 | 642 |
| 2005 | #425 | 730 |
| 2010 | #308 | 1,040 |
| 2015 | #271 | 1,178 |
| 2020 | #197 | 1,560 |
| 2024 | **#183** | **1,639** |

Phoebe has been climbing for 30 years and is still climbing in 2024. It's now at its highest rank ever in the SSA data.

Why? Because Phoebe hits the vintage-revival sweet spot. It's a Greek mythological name (see [mythological names](/blog/mythological-names.html)), it's long enough to feel substantial, it has an unusual spelling that feels distinctive without being unpronounceable, and it predates the Friends era enough to not feel like a "TV character name." The F sound is rare in top-100 names. Parents who discover Phoebe today may not even be thinking about Phoebe Buffay — they're finding a distinctive Greek name that has a cool sound.

Compare this to Chandler, which is unmistakably a Friends tribute, and Phoebe, which has independent appeal beyond the show.

## Ross: nearly extinct

**[Ross](/name/ross.html)** — Boys #1,359, 139 babies in 2024.

Ross was never a strong name — it was #311 in 1994 before the show, already a minor name for boys. The show didn't help.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #311 | 838 |
| 2000 | #434 | 561 |
| 2005 | #672 | 313 |
| 2010 | #922 | 219 |
| 2015 | #1,165 | 165 |
| 2020 | #1,289 | 155 |
| 2024 | **#1,359** | **139** |

139 babies named Ross in all of America in 2024. The name is effectively disappearing. Ross was a Scottish surname-as-given-name that peaked around the 1960s-1970s; by 1994 it was already declining, and the cultural association with Ross Geller (a character defined by divorces and being the "nerdy one") didn't reverse it.

This is an interesting contrast with Chandler, which at least got a brief boost.

## Chandler: the show's biggest direct effect

**[Chandler](/name/chandler.html)** — Boys #738, 348 babies in 2024.

Chandler is the most direct evidence of the show's naming impact, because the name's trajectory maps almost exactly onto Friends' cultural lifecycle.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1990 | #1,600s | ~50 |
| 1994 | #349 | 704 |
| 1997 | #165 | ~2,400 |
| 2000 | **#174** | **2,131** |
| 2005 | #340 | 870 |
| 2010 | #495 | 524 |
| 2015 | #598 | 488 |
| 2024 | #738 | 348 |

Before Friends, Chandler was a rare occupational surname (candle-maker). The show turned it into a real given name. The 1994-2000 surge from obscurity to #174 is essentially all Friends. Then as the show aged and cultural memory moved on, the name declined. By 2024 it's at #738 and still falling.

Chandler is a case study in TV-driven name boosts that don't stick: the name rose because of the character and is fading as the character feels more dated. Compare to Phoebe, which rose more slowly but has independent roots. See [pop-culture-names](/blog/pop-culture-names.html) for more examples.

## Joey: stuck in the middle

**[Joey](/name/joey.html)** — Boys #888, 266 babies in 2024.

Joey occupies an interesting position: it's a nickname-as-given-name (see [nickname names](/blog/nickname-names.html)) that peaked slightly earlier than Friends' cultural impact.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #414 | 554 |
| 2000 | #485 | 481 |
| 2005 | #510 | 482 |
| 2010 | #612 | 405 |
| 2015 | #717 | 369 |
| 2024 | #888 | 266 |

Joey has been declining slowly for 30 years. It's a friendly, approachable name that reads as a working-class nickname — Joey doesn't sound like a professional or a CEO, which may be part of its gradual decline as parents increasingly aspire to names that "age well" into adulthood. Joey works for a kid and a sitcom character; it's less obvious as a name for a 45-year-old.

---

## What Friends actually did to naming

The aggregate picture: *Friends* ran during the peak of Rachel and Monica (names that were already mainstream), gave Chandler a temporary boost, had minimal effect on Joey and Ross, and has had no role in Phoebe's recent rise (which is driven by independent vintage appeal).

The show's most lasting naming legacy may be **Chandler** — the one name that demonstrably rose *because* of the show, rather than coinciding with it. And Chandler is now declining back toward obscurity, which is the typical pattern for TV-tribute names.

The counterintuitive lesson: the name most associated with the show's cult favorite character (Phoebe) is the only one climbing in 2024 — and it's climbing *despite* the show, not because of it. Phoebe Buffay may have introduced the name to a generation, but today's parents are finding a Greek mythological name with roots, not a sitcom character's name.
