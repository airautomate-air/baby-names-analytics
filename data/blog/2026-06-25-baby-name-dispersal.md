In 1980, Jennifer was given to 3.52% of all girls born in the United States — one in every 28. The name was so dominant that a kindergarten teacher in 1985 might reasonably have had three Jennifers in a class of 25. The same classroom in 2024 would be unlikely to contain any two children with the same name at all.

No girls' name in 2024 reaches 1% of births. The most popular girls' name, **[Olivia](/name/olivia.html)**, captures 0.91% — roughly one in 110. #5 **[Mia](/name/mia.html)** captures 0.75%. Even the top 10 combined — Olivia, Emma, Amelia, Charlotte, Mia, Sophia, Isabella, Evelyn, Ava, Sofia — account for only 7.1% of all girl births. In 1980, the top 10 accounted for 17.1%.

American parents are naming their children from a far wider range of choices than at any point in the last century.

## The full picture, 1980–2024

| Year | #1 Name | #1 Pct | Top 10 share | Top 50 share | Unique names |
|------|---------|--------|--------------|--------------|--------------|
| 1980 | Jennifer | 3.52% | 17.1% | 41.9% | 12,162 |
| 1990 | Jessica | 2.45% | 16.0% | 39.5% | 15,233 |
| 2000 | Emily | 1.43% | 10.2% | 30.1% | 17,659 |
| 2010 | Isabella | 1.29% | 8.9% | 23.7% | 19,837 |
| 2015 | Emma | 1.15% | 8.4% | 23.3% | 19,128 |
| 2020 | Olivia | 1.09% | 7.9% | 22.2% | 17,517 |
| 2024 | Olivia | 0.91% | 7.1% | 21.1% | 17,661 |

Girls' naming is more dispersed than boys' naming in every year — there are more unique girls' names than boys' names in the data. But boys' naming has followed the same trajectory.

| Year | #1 Name | #1 Pct | Top 10 share | Top 50 share |
|------|---------|--------|--------------|--------------|
| 1980 | Michael | 3.85% | 23.6% | 57.7% |
| 1990 | Michael | 3.18% | 19.5% | 52.3% |
| 2000 | Jacob | 1.76% | 13.4% | 41.1% |
| 2010 | Jacob | 1.15% | 9.0% | 32.1% |
| 2020 | Liam | 1.15% | 7.9% | 26.3% |
| 2024 | Liam | 1.29% | 8.0% | 25.1% |

Boys' dispersal has been slightly less complete: **[Liam](/name/liam.html)** still captures 1.29% of boys' births in 2024, staying just above the 1% threshold. But in 1980, the top 10 boys' names captured 23.6% of all male births — nearly one in four boys had one of ten names. In 2024 it is 8%.

## What the 1980 #1 name would rank today

In 1980, Jennifer was given to 58,378 girls. That is the largest single-name count for any girl born that year.

**[Olivia](/name/olivia.html)** in 2024 received 14,718 births. That number would have ranked Olivia approximately #18 in the 1980 data — below Crystal (14,253, #18 in 1980) and just above Kelly (13,710, #19).

The current #1 girls' name, in absolute terms, would not have broken the 1980 top 15.

For boys: **[Michael](/name/michael.html)** in 1980 received 68,687 births, a figure that has never been approached since. In 2024, no boys' name comes close to that — Liam at #1 received 22,164. The single most popular boys' name today receives about 32% of what the most popular name received 44 years ago.

## Why the dispersal?

**Population diversification.** The US population in 1980 was less ethnically diverse, and naming traditions were less varied. As Hispanic, Asian, and other naming traditions have become more prominent — names like **[Mateo](/name/mateo.html)**, **[Luna](/name/luna.html)**, **[Camila](/name/camila.html)**, and **[Santiago](/name/santiago.html)** — the pool of names drawing significant numbers has widened substantially.

**More unique girls' names.** The number of unique girls' names in the SSA data grew from 12,162 in 1980 to a peak of 19,837 in 2010, reflecting the rise in invented, respelled, and culturally diverse names. Even after declining slightly (to 17,661 in 2024), the pool remains vastly larger than it was 40 years ago.

**The "common name" stigma.** Parents increasingly treat name choice as individual expression. A name that appears too often — perceived as "everyone's name" — gets avoided. The dispersal is partly self-reinforcing: as no single name captures attention as "the name of the moment," there is no convergence point.

**The generational collapse.** The names that drove concentration in earlier decades — Michael holding #1 for 43 of 44 years from 1954 to 1998; Jennifer holding #1 for 15 consecutive years — had unusually long periods at the top. No recent name has maintained #1 for more than a few years (Liam's run beginning 2017 is the longest current streak), and none carries the same share.

---

## One implication

The 2024 kindergarten class is statistically more name-diverse than any comparable cohort in recorded US data. The probability that any two randomly selected children in a classroom share a name has dropped substantially since 1980. The Jennifer problem — the awkwardness of being one of four Jennifers in your class — is largely gone.

The dispersal does not appear to be reversing. The top 50 girls' name share has fallen every decade since 1980. The unique name count, while lower than its 2010 peak, remains far above its 1980 level. The structural trend is toward more variety, not less.
