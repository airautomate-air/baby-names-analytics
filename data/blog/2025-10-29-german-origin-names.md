German is one of the great naming languages of the Western world — and most Americans are using it without knowing. The Germanic roots that gave English speakers William, Emma, Charles, and Henry are the same roots running through medieval German, Dutch, and the Frankish kingdoms that shaped Europe. Recognisably "German" names like Otto and Greta sit alongside names so thoroughly absorbed into English that their origins are invisible.

## German names in the US today

### William — #5 boys in 2024

One of the most durable names in American history, and thoroughly Germanic at its core. From the Old High German *Willahelm* — *willa* (will, desire) + *helm* (helmet, protection). The Normans brought it to England in 1066; it's been top-ten virtually ever since. [William](/name/william.html) feels classic without feeling old: it belongs to Shakespeare, four US presidents, and a future king simultaneously.

### Henry — #9 boys in 2024

From the Old High German *Heimirich* — *heim* (home) + *rīhhi* (ruler, powerful). Eight English kings, countless statesmen, and now a generation of American children have carried it. [Henry](/name/henry.html) sits at the intersection of the formal and the friendly: the full name sounds weighty, the nickname Hank sounds entirely American.

### Milo — #97 boys in 2024

A name with deep Germanic roots — from the Old High German *Milo* or *Mild*, possibly related to the Slavic *mil* (gracious), but long established in the German-speaking world. It has a lightness that its heavier Germanic cousins lack. [Milo](/name/milo.html) has been rising sharply; it sits in the sweet spot of distinctive but pronounceable.

### Charles — #50 boys in 2024

From the Germanic *Karl*, meaning "free man" — the same root as the name Charlemagne, the Frankish king who unified much of Western Europe. Karl became Charles in French and English; Carlos in Spanish; Carlo in Italian. The name has been nearly continuous in English use for over a millennium. [Charles](/name/charles.html) is formal but never stuffy, and the nickname Charlie has its own independent momentum.

### Emmett — #148 boys in 2024

A Germanic name with the element *ermen* (whole, universal) — the same root as Emma. Emmett arrived in English via the Old English *Emmet* and has a long Irish history too. In twenty-first century America, [Emmett](/name/emmett.html) feels like the ideal compromise between old-fashioned weight and modern approachability. It's the name for parents who want something vintage without the grandpa associations.

### Walter — #223 boys in 2024

From the Old High German *Walthari* — *wald* (rule) + *heri* (army). Walter was enormously common in medieval England and faded through most of the twentieth century. It's coming back as part of the broader resurrection of solid, underused names. [Walter](/name/walter.html) sits in interesting territory: old enough to feel fresh, famous enough (Walter White, Walter Cronkite) to carry cultural weight.

### Otto — #355 boys in 2024

One of the most recognisably German names in English use — Old High German *Odo* or *Otto*, meaning "wealth, fortune." It was the name of the Holy Roman Emperor Otto I. In the US it spent most of the twentieth century invisible, but [Otto](/name/otto.html) has been climbing steadily since 2010, part of the short-O vowel revival (Oscar, Oliver, Orion). Strong, palindromic, impossible to shorten.

### Frederick — #432 boys in 2024

From the Old High German *Fridurih* — *frid* (peace) + *rih* (ruler). Frederick the Great of Prussia made it synonymous with Enlightenment military genius. In the US it peaked in the late nineteenth century and has been rare since. [Frederick](/name/frederick.html) is genuinely underused — the nicknames Fred and Freddie are warm and accessible, and the full name sounds strikingly formal.

### Emma — #2 girls in 2024

The biggest name in America has Germanic roots. Emma comes from the Old High German element *ermen* meaning "whole" or "universal" — it was brought to England by Emma of Normandy, queen to two English kings. [Emma](/name/emma.html) is the rare name that sounds both timeless and contemporary, which is why it's been #1 or #2 for most of the past decade. Its popularity shows no sign of collapsing.

### Alice — #57 girls in 2024

From the Old French *Aalis*, itself from the Old High German *Adalheidis* — *adal* (noble) + *heid* (kind, type). The same root produces Adelaide. Alice shed its German scaffolding so thoroughly in England that most people think of it as purely English — but Carroll's Alice, Tennyson's Alice, and the medieval saints all trace back to the same Germanic source. [Alice](/name/alice.html) is a top-100 staple and still rising.

### Adelaide — #253 girls in 2024

Where Alice absorbed into English, Adelaide stayed visibly Germanic. From *Adalheidis*, the same root as Alice — "noble kind." Adelaide was a ninth-century queen of Italy and later Holy Roman Empress; Saint Adelaide was one of the most venerated women of medieval Europe. In modern America, [Adelaide](/name/adelaide.html) feels like the elaborate sister of Alice — longer, rarer, equally rooted.

### Elsa — #257 girls in 2024

A German short form of Elisabeth — which itself comes from the Hebrew *Elisheba*. Elsa has Germanic associations reaching back to medieval legend (Elsa of Brabant in Wagner's *Lohengrin*) but in American popular consciousness it now belongs almost entirely to Frozen. The Disney effect is real: [Elsa](/name/elsa.html) shot up after 2013 and has held its ground. Frozen or not, it's a genuine Germanic name with centuries of use.

### Greta — #303 girls in 2024

A German short form of Margarete — itself from the Greek *margarites* (pearl), but naturalised in German for centuries. Greta Garbo brought it to Hollywood glamour in the 1930s; Greta Thunberg gave it a new international profile in the 2010s. [Greta](/name/greta.html) is short, serious, and unhyphenated — it has the directness that many German names share.

### Ida — #348 girls in 2024

From the Germanic element *id*, meaning "work" or "labour" — or possibly from the Proto-Germanic *idis*, meaning "woman." Ida was enormously popular in the late nineteenth century (propelled partly by Tennyson's long poem *The Princess*) and faded through most of the twentieth. It's quietly reviving. [Ida](/name/ida.html) is among the shortest, oldest, and most underused of the Germanic names currently returning to fashion.

## The Germanic thread

German-origin names share some qualities that explain their endurance in English:

- **Weight and substance** — Germanic names tend to have hard consonants and compound roots (*heim* + *rīhhi*, *willa* + *helm*) that give them a solidity other naming traditions lack
- **Short-form culture** — almost every long Germanic name generates a beloved short form: William → Will/Bill, Frederick → Fred, Adelaide → Addie/Ada, Emma (already short) → none needed
- **Invisible roots** — many Germanic names have been in English use so long that their origins are simply unknown to most parents. William, Emma, and Charles don't feel "German" in America; they feel foundational

The Germanic naming tradition didn't arrive in one wave — it came with the Anglo-Saxons in the fifth century, the Normans in 1066, and again with German and Dutch immigrants in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. It's been part of English naming for 1,500 years.
