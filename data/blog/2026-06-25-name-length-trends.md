In 1990, nine of the top twenty girls' names had seven or more letters. Jessica. Brittany. Samantha. Stephanie. Jennifer. Courtney. Danielle. Heather. Melissa. All were in the top twenty. All have since fallen out of the top 300.

In 2024, nine of the top twenty girls' names have four or fewer letters. Emma. Mia. Ava. Luna. Nora. Zoe. Ella. Lily. Aria.

The shift is not subtle. It shows up clearly in the SSA data going back to 1980.

---

## The numbers: average name length, 1980–2024

Measured as the average letter count of the top 100 names, weighted by birth counts.

| Year | Girls avg (top 100) | Boys avg (top 100) | Girls 7+ letter share | Boys 7+ letter share |
|------|--------------------|--------------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| 1980 | 6.31 | 6.00 | 47.3% | 34.4% |
| 1990 | 6.61 | 6.07 | 51.6% | 36.0% |
| 2000 | 6.40 | 5.97 | 41.9% | 32.2% |
| 2010 | 6.04 | 5.83 | 36.5% | 26.3% |
| 2020 | 5.79 | 5.77 | 29.9% | 26.5% |
| 2024 | 5.64 | 5.61 | 24.7% | 23.6% |

The peak for girls was 1990, when more than half of all births in the top 100 went to names of seven letters or longer. In 2024, that share is under 25%. For boys, the trend is similar but started from a lower base.

The "7+ letter share" column measures what fraction of births in the top 100 went to names that long. It is not a count of names — it is a count of babies. The trend reflects both which names parents choose and how many babies each name gets.

## What left and what arrived

The girls' names that were top 20 in 1990 but are no longer in the top 100 in 2024 are a roll call of seven-letter-and-up names: **[Jessica](/name/jessica.html)** (#1 then, #574 now), **[Brittany](/name/brittany.html)** (#3 then, #791 now), **[Samantha](/name/samantha.html)** (#5 then, #127 now), **[Stephanie](/name/stephanie.html)** (#7 then, #533 now), **[Jennifer](/name/jennifer.html)** (#8 then, #547 now), **[Courtney](/name/courtney.html)** (#17 then, #1,978 now), **[Danielle](/name/danielle.html)** (#18 then, #523 now), **[Heather](/name/heather.html)** (#19 then, #1,352 now), **[Melissa](/name/melissa.html)** (#20 then, #378 now).

What replaced them in the 2024 top 20: **[Emma](/name/emma.html)** (4 letters, #2), **[Mia](/name/mia.html)** (3 letters, #5), **[Ava](/name/ava.html)** (3 letters, #9), **[Luna](/name/luna.html)** (4 letters, #13), **[Nora](/name/nora.html)** (4 letters, #22), **[Lily](/name/lily.html)** (4 letters, #24), **[Zoe](/name/zoe.html)** (3 letters, #29), **[Ella](/name/ella.html)** (4 letters, #30).

For boys, the pattern is similar but less extreme. The 1990 top 20 included **[Christopher](/name/christopher.html)** (#2, now #61), **[Matthew](/name/matthew.html)** (#3, now #33), **[Nicholas](/name/nicholas.html)** (#14, now #118), **[Jonathan](/name/jonathan.html)** (#17, now #83), **[Brandon](/name/brandon.html)** (#19, now #230). These have not crashed to the same degree as the girls' long names, but all have given ground to shorter alternatives.

The 2024 top 20 boys' names are dominated by **[Liam](/name/liam.html)** (4 letters, #1), **[Noah](/name/noah.html)** (4 letters, #2), **[Levi](/name/levi.html)** (4 letters, #12), **[Ezra](/name/ezra.html)** (4 letters, #13), **[Jack](/name/jack.html)** (4 letters, #15).

## The short-name share over time

| Year | Girls ≤4 letters (share of top 100 births) | Boys ≤4 letters |
|------|---------------------------------------------|-----------------|
| 1980 | 12.4% | 15.8% |
| 1990 | 5.5% | 16.5% |
| 2000 | 7.1% | 17.2% |
| 2010 | 15.8% | 16.1% |
| 2020 | 24.9% | 19.7% |
| 2024 | 26.2% | 24.6% |

Girls' naming was actually more concentrated in longer names in the 1990 peak before reversing. Boys' short-name share stayed relatively flat through the 1980s–2010s and then accelerated from 2015 onward, driven by **[Liam](/name/liam.html)** and **[Noah](/name/noah.html)** cementing the #1 and #2 positions.

## Why shorter?

Several factors likely converge:

**The generational association of the long names.** Jessica, Brittany, Jennifer, and Courtney are permanently associated with parents and aunts born in the 1970s–1990s. Parents naming children in the 2020s avoid names that feel dated to their own generation. The avoidance applies especially to the long, elaborate names that were overrepresented in that cohort.

**International influence.** The Hispanic naming tradition (which values names like Mia, Luna, Sofia, Camila) and the European short-name aesthetic (Emma is popular across the English-speaking world, Mia across Western Europe) have influenced US naming as the US population has become more ethnically diverse.

**The sound preference shift.** Short names with open vowels — Ava, Mia, Luna, Zoe — carry a different phonetic quality than the longer names they replaced. The two-vowel short names that dominate 2024's top 10 (Olivia excepted) are distinct from the consonant-heavy long names that dominated 1990.

The average length of a top-100 name has now fallen for four consecutive decades for girls. There is no reason in the current data to expect the trend to reverse.
