Look at the boys top 50 in 2024 and count the names that end in N.

Liam. Noah. Ethan. Logan. Owen. Julian. Aiden. Sebastian. Ryan. Nathan. Ian. Evan. Hudson. Mason. Jackson. Weston. Leon. Holden. Roman. Adrian.

Count them: roughly 20 of the top 50 boys names end in N. That's 40% of the top tier dominated by a single phoneme. No vowel, no other consonant comes close.

This isn't random. The N-ending has become the defining sound of American boys naming — a pattern that has been building for decades and shows no sign of reversing.

## Liam: the N-ending that reached #1

**[Liam](/name/liam.html)** — Boys #1, 20,456 babies in 2024.

Liam is the Irish short form of William and has been the #1 boys name in America since 2017. It's also an N-ending name that barely registered in American naming before the 1990s.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #316 | 871 |
| 2000 | #179 | 2,085 |
| 2004 | #97 | 4,712 |
| 2008 | #41 | 9,086 |
| 2010 | #20 | 12,536 |
| 2014 | #2 | 17,303 |
| 2017 | **#1** | **18,728** |
| 2024 | #1 | 20,456 |

Liam's rise was partly driven by celebrity — Liam Neeson's career peak (1994-2010) and later One Direction's Liam Payne (2010s) kept the name in cultural view. But the underlying driver is phonetic: Liam is short, punchy, ends in the reliable -m-n sequence, and has a clear Irish heritage without feeling exclusively ethnic.

## Ethan: the N-ending that peaked and held

**[Ethan](/name/ethan.html)** — Boys #16, 12,981 babies in 2024.

Ethan was the first N-ending name to hit #1 in the modern era, reaching the top spot in 2008. Unlike some names that collapse after peaking, Ethan has held on surprisingly well.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #80 | 6,498 |
| 2000 | #20 | 11,045 |
| 2004 | #3 | 17,867 |
| 2008 | **#1** | **22,492** |
| 2012 | #4 | 16,932 |
| 2014 | #10 | 13,850 |
| 2020 | #14 | 12,984 |
| 2024 | #16 | 12,981 |

Ethan's peak at #1 in 2008 coincided with *Ethan Frome* being standard school reading, *Mission: Impossible* (Ethan Hunt), and a general taste for short, strong, biblical-adjacent names. Its decline from #1 to #16 is slow — there's no catastrophic cultural event dragging it down, just the natural generational aging curve. Ethan will likely remain in the top 30 for another decade.

## Logan: the hero name

**[Logan](/name/logan.html)** — Boys #21, 11,340 babies in 2024.

Logan is one of the most interesting N-ending names because its rise coincides almost exactly with the X-Men franchise. Wolverine's real name is James Logan.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #166 | 2,062 |
| 2000 | #122 | 3,215 |
| 2004 | #82 | 5,611 |
| 2010 | #25 | 10,906 |
| 2014 | #16 | 12,940 |
| 2018 | #15 | 13,016 |
| 2022 | #22 | 11,261 |
| 2024 | **#21** | **11,340** |

Logan's rise from #166 to the top 25 happened in the 2000s-2010s, when the X-Men films were at their peak cultural influence (*X-Men* 2000, *X2* 2003, *X-Men Origins: Wolverine* 2009, *The Wolverine* 2013, *Logan* 2017). The name also works as a Scottish surname crossover independent of the superhero association, so it has dual appeal.

## Owen: the oldest name on the list

**[Owen](/name/owen.html)** — Boys #44, 7,118 babies in 2024.

Owen is an ancient Welsh name (from the Latin *Eugenius*, meaning "well-born") that has been in continuous use for over a thousand years. It predates the N-ending trend by centuries.

| Year | Rank | Babies |

| 2010 | #56 | 6,534 |
| 2014 | #28 | 10,011 |

Owen peaked around 2014 and is in slight decline. Owen Wilson's career peak in the 2000s (and the shared surname-name quality) contributed to its rise. Owen sits in a comfortable tier — too common to be rare, not common enough to feel saturated. The Welsh connection makes it feel grounded in old tradition without being stuffy.

## Why does the N-ending dominate?

Linguistically, the nasal consonant /n/ is one of the most sonically satisfying word endings in English. It's resonant without being harsh — think of how words like "golden," "garden," "moon," and "dawn" feel comfortable at the end of a phrase. Names ending in N have a closing finality that feels complete.

The pattern runs through the decades:
- **1960s-70s**: Jason, Ryan, Brian, Kevin, Nathan
- **1980s-90s**: Brendan, Ethan, Logan, Dylan
- **2000s-10s**: Aiden, Jayden, Brayden, Mason, Jackson, Grayson
- **2020s**: Liam (at #1), Weston, Roman, Hudson, Leon

Each decade's N-ending names have a different flavor — the Irish names (Liam, Aiden, Sean), the surname-names (Logan, Mason, Hudson), the classical names (Julian, Sebastian, Adrian), the nature names (Wren, Robin) — but the ending stays constant.
