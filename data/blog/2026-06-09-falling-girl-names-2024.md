The four years between 2020 and 2024 reshuffled the girl-name chart almost as much as they reshuffled the rest of life. These are the ten names that fell furthest — all of them top-300 girl picks in 2020, all of them well outside the top 350 today.

## The list

1. **[Alexa](/name/alexa.html)** — from #230 in 2020 to **#806 in 2024** (−576). The biggest fall on the list, and it has an obvious culprit: a smart-speaker named Alexa moved into millions of US homes. Parents stopped naming their daughters after the family appliance.
2. **[Aubree](/name/aubree.html)** — from #156 to **#408** (−252). The "-ee" ending wave (Aubree, Bailee, Marlee) is receding; the parents picking variant spellings have moved on to other vowel sounds.
3. **[Mckenzie](/name/mckenzie.html)** — from #281 to **#493** (−212). All the Mc- and Mac- prefixed surname-names are sliding together.
4. **[Brooklynn](/name/brooklynn.html)** — from #262 to **#468** (−206). The double-N spelling is fading faster than plain Brooklyn.
5. **[Alyssa](/name/alyssa.html)** — from #199 to **#399** (−200). A 1990s/2000s mainstay continues its long graceful descent.
6. **[Raegan](/name/raegan.html)** — from #259 to **#458** (−199). Reagan/Raegan spelled either way is sliding; the Raegan variant is sliding faster.
7. **[Cali](/name/cali.html)** — from #296 to **#495** (−199). Short geography names rose hard in the 2010s and are now picking the next destinations.
8. **[Taylor](/name/taylor.html)** — from #162 to **#353** (−191). One of the great cross-gender surname-names of the '90s, still trending down despite the cultural megastar attached to it.
9. **[Trinity](/name/trinity.html)** — from #225 to **#407** (−182). Virtue and religious names ran hot in the 2000s and are now cooling.
10. **[Finley](/name/finley.html)** — from #201 to **#365** (−164). Finley peaked around 2018–2020 for girls and is now drifting; the boys' Finley is holding steadier.

## What's the pattern?

Three themes run through the list:

- **Variant spellings are losing ground.** Aubree, Mckenzie, Brooklynn, Raegan — four of ten. The 2010s appetite for distinguishing your child via spelling has clearly cooled.
- **2000s-era word and virtue names are sliding.** Trinity, Cali, Taylor — names that anchored that era are giving way to longer, softer, more vintage choices (Charlotte, Eleanor, Hazel — see our [vintage comeback post](/blog/vintage-girl-names-comeback.html)).
- **A few have clear external causes.** Alexa is the clearest cultural collision in modern name data. Once Amazon launched the device in 2014, the name's slide accelerated; the post-2020 nosedive is the long tail of that decision.

## More names to watch

Outside the top 10 fallers, several other girl names slipped meaningfully between 2020 and 2024:

- **[Bailee](/name/bailee.html)** and **[Marlee](/name/marlee.html)** — the same "-ee" ending trend that hit Aubree. Both names are down significantly from their 2018–2020 peaks.
- **[Paisley](/name/paisley.html)** — still top 50, but the climb has stopped and the slide has begun. The country-music aesthetic that powered it is fading.
- **[Destiny](/name/destiny.html)** — a 1990s–2000s virtue-name that peaked around 2001 at #12 and has been declining since. It's now outside the top 400.
- **[Ryleigh](/name/ryleigh.html)** — the "-leigh" spelling variant of Riley is falling faster than plain Riley, mirroring the Brooklynn vs Brooklyn pattern.
- **[Miley](/name/miley.html)** — the celebrity-driven spike from the late 2000s has fully unwound. The name is still present but has lost its momentum.
- **[Autumn](/name/autumn.html)** — a subtle drop; this nature name was very consistent through the 2010s and is now softening, possibly displaced by other nature names (Hazel, Violet, Ivy) that have a more vintage feel.

## The Alexa story in detail

Alexa deserves its own paragraph because it's the most dramatic single-cause decline in the modern SSA dataset. The name was #54 for girls as recently as 2015 — well into the top 100. Amazon launched its Alexa voice assistant in late 2014, and the name's descent accelerated from that point. By 2019 it was below #200. By 2024 it's at #806.

The word-collision problem is almost impossible to overcome once it takes hold. Every time a parent says "Alexa, play music" or "Alexa, set a timer," the name gains another layer of appliance association. The brand's decision to use a previously feminine name created an accidental veto on the name for a decade.

The trajectory of Alexa mirrors what happened to names that collided with news events historically — the difference is that Amazon's Alexa is a daily active presence in tens of millions of homes, not a one-time association that fades.

## What parents are choosing instead

Parents who might have chosen these names in 2018–2020 are redirecting toward:

- **Standard spellings over variants**: Brooklyn instead of Brooklynn, Reagan instead of Raegan, Aubrey instead of Aubree. The drive to customize via spelling has cooled considerably.
- **Vintage girl names**: Charlotte, Eleanor, Hazel, Violet, and Rosalie are absorbing demand that once flowed to Trinity and Cali. The aesthetic has shifted from "fresh and modern" to "timeless and romantic."
- **Longer melodic names**: Arabella, Isadora, Evangeline, Seraphina — names in the four-syllable range that feel luxurious rather than edgy. These are picking up share from the short, punchy names of the 2010s.
- **Nature names with vintage feel**: Ivy, Willow, Flora, Marigold — nature names are still popular, but the direction has shifted from geography-coded (Cali, Brooklyn) to plant-and-flower coded.

## The regional picture

The -ee spelling variant names (Aubree, Bailee, Mckenzie) were disproportionately popular in the South and Mountain West, the same regions that drove the K-spelling boys' wave. As those regional tastes shift, they pull national numbers.

Taylor's decline is notable partly because of Taylor Swift's cultural omnipresence — you'd expect the opposite. But the data suggests the opposite dynamic: Swift's fame may have made the name feel spoken for, or parents may simply feel that their daughter will share a name with an artist who is already iconic in a way that creates unwanted comparisons.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Alexa ever coming back?**
It's hard to see a path while Amazon's product is a household staple. The brand would need to rebrand its assistant, or a generation would need to pass for the association to fade. The closest historical parallel is when a product name successfully detaches from its original meaning — unlikely here because "Alexa" as an assistant is still actively marketed. The Greek-origin Alexandra, from which Alexa derives, continues to rank respectably at around #160.

**Why is Taylor falling even with Taylor Swift's cultural influence?**
Counter-intuitively, an extremely famous bearer can suppress a name by making it feel "taken." Parents who loved Taylor in 2000 as a fresh surname-name now associate it so completely with the artist that naming a daughter Taylor feels like a tribute rather than a personal choice. The same dynamic affected Jennifer after Jennifer Aniston and Jennifer Lopez defined that era.

**Are variant spellings permanently out of fashion?**
Probably not permanently, but the current wave is clearly cooling. The 2000s–2010s appetite for respellings was partly driven by a belief that a unique spelling would make a child stand out. Research and lived experience suggest it mostly creates spelling friction without uniqueness, and naming culture has absorbed that lesson.

**Which 2020-era girl names are next to fall?**
Watch Paisley, Rylee (and its variants), and any name that became popular primarily through a single cultural moment. Names that lack deep roots or multiple cultural supports tend to fall when the wave that lifted them recedes.
