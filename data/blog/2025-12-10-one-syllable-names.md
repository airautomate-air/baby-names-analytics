American baby naming has split into two extremes. On one end: Olivia (4 syllables), Amelia (4), Isabella (4), Theodore (3), Alexander (4). On the other: Jack (1), James (1), Kai (1), Maeve (1), Wren (1), June (1). See [long and elaborate names](/blog/long-baby-names.html) for the full analysis of the 4-syllable side.

The two-syllable names that dominated the 1980s and 1990s (Jason, Tyler, Ashley, Chelsea, Brandon) are declining. The extremes — very long and very short — are both rising. This is the bimodal era of baby naming.

---

## The classic one-syllable boys, revived

The most striking finding in the boys' data: the ancient one-syllable names are recovering, after decades of decline.

### James — #5 (11,793 babies)

**[James](/name/james.html)** — English form of Hebrew *Yaakov* (supplanter). James was #3 in 1960, fell to #18 by 2000 as two-syllable names (Jason, Tyler, Bryan) took over, and has now recovered to #5. James may be the most consistently top-10 name in American history — it's been in the top 10 for most of the past 200 years, with a brief mid-range hiatus in the 1990s-2000s.

### Jack — #15 (8,434 babies)

**[Jack](/name/jack.html)** — Medieval English diminutive of John (via Jankin → Jackin → Jack). Jack was #85 in 1960, fell as low as #46 in 2000, and has climbed sharply to #15 in 2024. Jack is now more popular than it's been in 50 years — the vintage British feel (Jack the Ripper, Jack Nicholson, Captain Jack Sparrow) and the punchy one-syllable sound both contribute.

### John — #21 (8,047 babies)

**[John](/name/john.html)** — Hebrew *Yochanan*: "Yahweh is gracious." John was #4 in 1960 and #14 in 2000. It has fallen slightly to #21 — but for a name that was literally the most common boys' name in the English-speaking world for most of recorded history, #21 is still a strong position. See [names meaning grace](/blog/names-meaning-grace.html).

### Luke — #34 (7,039 babies)

**[Luke](/name/luke.html)** — Greek *Loukas*, from Latin *Lucas* (from *lux*, light). Luke was #384 in 1960 — a rare name then — and has climbed to #34 in 2024. Luke Skywalker (1977), Luke Bryan (country star), and the New Testament Gospel of Luke all maintain different associations. The Latin *lux* connection links Luke to the broader "names meaning light" cluster. See [names meaning light](/blog/names-meaning-light.html).

## The rising new one-syllable boys

### Kai — #76 (4,276 babies)

**[Kai](/name/kai.html)** — Multiple origins: Hawaiian *kai* (sea), Welsh *Cai* (the name of Sir Kay, King Arthur's foster brother), Frisian *Kai* (warrior), Japanese *kai* (ocean, or ash wood). Kai's multicultural availability gives it unusual flexibility — it works in Hawaiian, Japanese, Welsh, Scandinavian, and African American naming traditions.

| Year | Rank |
|------|------|
| 2000 | #403 |
| 2010 | #205 |
| 2015 | #145 |
| 2020 | #93 |
| 2024 | **#76** |

Kai is also one of the few one-syllable names that works well for both boys and girls — though the boys' use is significantly more common.

### Jude — #156 (2,284 babies)

**[Jude](/name/jude.html)** — Hebrew *Yehudah* (praised). Jude the Apostle; the Beatles' "Hey Jude" (1968). Jude Law's career in the early 2000s accelerated the name's visibility. At #156, Jude is well-established as a one-syllable boys' name.

### Ace — #165 (2,203 babies)

**[Ace](/name/ace.html)** — English, from Latin *as* (a unit, a single point). Ace went from #1,924 in 2000 to #165 in 2024 — one of the most dramatic climbs among short names. The word meaning ("to ace something," "ace in the hole") gives the name a success/achievement association that parents find appealing.

### Rhett — #174 (2,134 babies)

**[Rhett](/name/rhett.html)** — Dutch *de Raedt*, used as a first name primarily through Rhett Butler, the male lead in *Gone with the Wind* (1939). Rhett was #676 in 2000 and has climbed to #174 in 2024 — driven partly by the country and Southern naming aesthetic. See [country western names](/blog/country-western-names.html).

### Knox — #209 (1,701 babies)

**[Knox](/name/knox.html)** — Scottish/English surname (from Old English *cnoc*, a hilltop). John Knox was the founder of Scottish Presbyterianism. Knox was #3,712 in 2000 (22 babies) and is now #209. Angelina Jolie and Brad Pitt named one of their twin sons **Knox Léon** in 2008 — a clear celebrity driver.

### Finn — #198 (1,791 babies)

**[Finn](/name/finn.html)** — Irish *Fionn* (fair, white). Finn McCool (*Fionn mac Cumhaill*) is the great hero of Irish mythology. Finn has been climbing steadily from #836 (2000) to #198 (2024). See [Irish names](/blog/irish-origin-names.html).

## One-syllable girls: ancient names returning

The one-syllable girls' story is largely about recovery: names that were common in the early 20th century, fell through the mid-century, and are coming back with vintage warmth.

### Maeve — #75 (3,098 babies)

**[Maeve](/name/maeve.html)** — Irish *Méabh* (intoxicating, she who intoxicates). Queen Maeve of Connacht is one of the great figures of Irish mythology. Maeve was #778 in 2000 and has shot to #75 in 2024:

| Year | Rank |
|------|------|
| 2000 | #778 |
| 2015 | #452 |
| 2020 | #173 |
| 2024 | **#75** |

Maeve is the most successful one-syllable girls' name rise in recent data. Its surge correlates with the Irish-name revival and the vintage aesthetic. The *Schitt's Creek* character Moira's granddaughter Maeve may have contributed; the TV show *Sex Education* (UK, 2019–2023) features a Maeve prominently.

### Grace — #40 (5,002 babies)

**[Grace](/name/grace.html)** — Latin *gratia* (grace). Grace is the most established one-syllable girls' name — it's been in the top 25 since the late 1990s. Grace Kelly (actress, Princess of Monaco) gave the name its modern glamour. At #40, it's slightly off its 2000s peak but remains foundational. See [names meaning grace](/blog/names-meaning-grace.html).

### Claire — #67 (3,329 babies)

**[Claire](/name/claire.html)** — French form of Latin *Clara* (clear, bright). Claire is stable in the 50–90 range, maintaining a position that reflects its enduring appeal without any dramatic recent surge.

### June — #152 (1,956 babies)

**[June](/name/june.html)** — From the Roman goddess Juno, via the Latin *Junius* (the month). June was #1,566 in 2000 and is now #152 — a steady, sustained climb. The month-as-name tradition gives June a seasonal freshness. See [season names](/blog/season-names.html).

### Ruth — #172 (1,767 babies)

**[Ruth](/name/ruth.html)** — Hebrew, generally translated as "friend" or "companion." Ruth was #339 in 2000 and has climbed slowly but consistently to #172. The resurgence of Ruth parallels the broader return of short, simple, Old Testament women's names (Eve, Leah, Naomi). Ruth Bader Ginsburg's prominence from 2013 to her 2020 death brought the name into cultural discussion; Justice Ginsburg became a popular cultural icon ("the Notorious RBG") specifically among parents of naming age.

### Wren — #213 (1,442 babies)

**[Wren](/name/wren.html)** — Old English *wrenna*, the small bird known for its surprisingly loud song. Wren was #6,200 in 2000 — essentially 18 babies per year — and is now #213 with 1,442:

| Year | Rank |
|------|------|
| 2000 | #6,200 |
| 2010 | #1,291 |
| 2015 | #706 |
| 2020 | #360 |
| 2024 | **#213** |

The largest single percentage rise of any one-syllable girls' name over this period. Wren has everything the current trend values: short, distinctive, nature-adjacent (it's a bird), and without any "celebrity" association that could make it feel dated.

## Why one-syllable names are rising

**The sound contrast.** When a name like Olivia (O-LIV-i-a) flows over four syllables, a sibling named Wren sounds intentionally stark and distinctive. One-syllable names read as confident and self-contained next to elaborate names.

**No nickname needed.** Long names come with built-in nicknames — Isabella → Bella, Theodore → Theo. One-syllable names give children exactly what's on their birth certificate: Kai is Kai, Wren is Wren. No shortening, no confusion.

**The surname connection.** Many one-syllable names share the phonetic quality of surnames: Knox, Rhett, Tate, Nash, Reed, Blake, Quinn. The surname-first-name trend overlaps significantly with one-syllable preference. See [surname names](/blog/surname-names.html).

**Vintage revival.** The classic one-syllable names (James, Jack, John, June, Ruth, Grace) are mostly traditional names returning after a mid-century decline. The "rediscovery" of simple names is part of the same impulse that's bringing back Theodore, Eleanor, and Hazel.
