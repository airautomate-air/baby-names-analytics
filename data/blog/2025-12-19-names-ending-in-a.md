Fourteen of those twenty-five names — 56% — end in the letter *a*.

In 1960, the top 25 also had many -a names: Linda, Donna, Patricia, Debra, Sandra, Barbara, Cynthia, Laura. But the total was 12 out of 25, and they were concentrated lower in the chart.

Something has changed. The -a ending has become the dominant sound in girls' naming to a degree that has no historical precedent in the SSA data.

---

## The 1960 vs 2024 split

In 1960, the -a names sounded like this:

> Linda, Donna, Lisa, Patricia, Debra, Cynthia, Sandra, Barbara, Brenda, Pamela, Teresa, Laura

In 2024, the -a names sound like this:

> Olivia, Emma, Amelia, Mia, Sophia, Isabella, Ava, Sofia, Camila, Luna, Aurora, Eliana, Nora, Gianna

The ending is the same letter. But these are different kinds of -a names.

The 1960 names are English and Germanic words with a feminine -a added: Linda (German *lind*, soft); Sandra (contracted from Alexandra); Pamela (invented by Philip Sidney for a 1580 poem); Debra (a spelling variant of Deborah, which ends in Hebrew *-ah*, not actually an -a). Many of them are English-language names that happen to end in the letter a.

The 2024 names are Romance, Italian, Spanish, and Latin names where the -a is the natural feminine form: Olivia (Latin *oliva*, olive); Sophia (Greek *sophia*, wisdom); Isabella (Italian feminine of Isaiah); Camila (Latin/Spanish); Gianna (Italian feminine diminutive of John); Luna (Latin, moon); Aurora (Latin, dawn).

In other words: the TYPE of -a name has shifted from constructed English -a names to imported Latinate names that end in -a organically.

## Why -ia names specifically

Of the fourteen -a names in the 2024 top 25, eleven end specifically in *-ia*:

- Olivia, Amelia, Mia, Sophia, Sofia, Eliana, Gianna, Camila (ends -a but from -ila), Luna, Aurora, Nora

The *-ia* ending is the classical Latin/Greek feminine suffix. *Sophia* (wisdom), *Olivia* (olive), *Amelia* (from Germanic but Latinized), *Victoria*, *Julia*, *Lydia*, *Lucia* — these are words that have been feminine in Latin for two thousand years. The sound isn't new; the taste for it is.

This is part of a broader romance-language influence on American naming: the Spanish and Italian cultural presence in American life has made names like Sofia, Camila, Isabella, and Gianna feel natural to English-speaking parents who might never have considered them before.

## The top -a names in 2024

**[Olivia](/name/olivia.html) — #1** (14,718 babies). The most popular girls name in the United States. Olivia has been #1 or #2 since 2018. It's also #1 in the UK, Canada, and Australia — the name with the widest Anglosphere dominance of any name in decades.

**[Emma](/name/emma.html) — #2** (13,485 babies). Emma is Germanic (*ermen*, whole, universal), not a Latinate -a name — but it fits the sound perfectly. Emma Woodhouse (*Emma*, Jane Austen, 1815) and Emma Watson (Hermione Granger) have kept the name literary and modern at once.

**[Amelia](/name/amelia.html) — #3** (12,740 babies). German origin (from *Amalia*), but Latinized and embraced by English speakers through Amelia Earhart and the *Amelia* children's books. Amelia has been top 10 since 2012.

**[Mia](/name/mia.html) — #5** (12,113 babies). Scandinavian and Italian diminutive of Maria. Short, clear, easy in any language. Mia Farrow; *La La Land*'s Mia Dolan; Mia in *The Princess Diaries*. Three letters, one of the most popular names in the English-speaking world.

**[Sophia](/name/sophia.html) — #6** (12,088 babies). Greek for wisdom (*sophia*). Sophia was #1 for five years running (2011–2015). Now at #6, but still 12,000 babies per year.

**[Isabella](/name/isabella.html) — #7** (10,770 babies). *Twilight*'s Bella Swan was named Isabella. Isabella was #1 in 2009 and 2010 at the peak of *Twilight* mania. Now at #7 — declining slowly but still huge.

**[Ava](/name/ava.html) — #9** (8,662 babies). Latin or Hebrew (various theories), but most likely popularized by Ava Gardner in the 1950s. Ava went from #180 in 1999 to #5 in 2005 — one of the fastest single-decade rises in American naming history — and has been in the top 10 ever since.

**[Sofia](/name/sofia.html) — #10** (8,094 babies). Spanish spelling of Sophia. Sofia is particularly popular in Hispanic communities and has been climbing independently. Sofia Vergara has kept the spelling visible.

**[Camila](/name/camila.html) — #11** (7,608 babies). Spanish feminine form of Camille. Camila Cabello (Grammy-winning Cuban-American singer, born 1997) has driven this spelling significantly — the English *Camilla* is at #540 while the Spanish *Camila* is at #11.

**[Luna](/name/luna.html) — #13** (7,135 babies). Latin for moon. Luna Lovegood in *Harry Potter*; Luna as the celestial name trend; Luna as Spanish for moon. Luna has climbed from #600+ in 2006 to #13 in 2024.

**[Aurora](/name/aurora.html) — #16** (6,917 babies). Latin for dawn. *Sleeping Beauty*'s Aurora; the northern lights. See [nature names](/blog/nature-names.html) and [mythological names](/blog/mythological-names.html).

**[Eliana](/name/eliana.html) — #18** (6,423 babies). Hebrew (God has answered) or Latin, depending on tradition. Eliana has been climbing rapidly — it was outside the top 150 as recently as 2015. The four-syllable cadence (E-li-AN-a) is shared with Evangelina and Anastasia.

**[Nora](/name/nora.html) — #22** (6,128 babies). Irish diminutive of Honora or Eleanor. Nora is vintage, short, and ends in -a.

**[Gianna](/name/gianna.html) — #23** (6,097 babies). Italian feminine of Giovanni (John). Gianna was pushed into the mainstream by Gianna Bryant — Kobe Bryant's daughter who died with him in the 2020 helicopter crash. The name was at #247 in 2019 and reached #59 by 2020. Now it's stable at #23.

## What the -a sound does

Why do parents gravitate toward -a endings for girls?

**It's strongly coded as feminine.** In Italian, Spanish, Latin, and Greek, -a is the feminine form of nouns and adjectives. English parents may not consciously know this, but they've absorbed the pattern: Sophia, Maria, Julia, Elena, Isabella all end in -a *because* they are feminine in their source language. The feminine signal is deep.

**It's open and soft.** The -a vowel ending leaves the mouth open and the sound carrying. Compare Emma and Charlotte: both end in a similar short syllable, but Charlotte closes with a hard -t while Emma floats. Linguists note that vowel-final names tend to be perceived as softer and warmer in English.

**It's globally accessible.** In most European and Latin American languages, the -a ending is neutral or specifically feminine. A name like Olivia, Sophia, or Emma is usable in Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, French, and German with minimal adaptation. For multicultural families or families imagining their child's global life, -a endings are a natural choice.

## The Charlotte exception

**[Charlotte](/name/charlotte.html)** — #4 (12,552 babies) — is the top-5 girls name that *doesn't* end in -a. Charlotte ends in a French -tte suffix. It's the only name in the top 5 that bucks the pattern. This is notable because Charlotte has been climbing faster than any traditional English girls name in the last decade — royal associations (Princess Charlotte) and classic English naming may be providing an alternative to the -ia wave.
