The year was 1914. Archduke Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo in June, triggering a war that would consume Europe. Woodrow Wilson was in the White House. The US was not yet in the war — that was three years away — and about 2.9 million babies were born. A remarkable number of them were named John or Mary.

Here's what the SSA data shows for 1914.

## What stands out

**John's dominance.** In 1914, roughly 1 in 30 boys born in the US was named John. In 2024, John is #21 with about 8,000 babies — still alive, but a shadow of its 1914 presence. No name has ever maintained the kind of singular dominance John held across the first half of the twentieth century.

**The boys' list has aged almost perfectly.** Ten of the 15 boys' names from 1914 are still inside the top 225 today. Five are back in the top 50. Henry — at #9 in 2024 — has made one of the most dramatic comebacks of any name in the dataset, going from #10 in 1914 to near-invisibility in the 1970s and back to the top 10 now.

**The girls' list is splitting.** The 1914 girls' names are falling into two camps: the genuinely timeless ones (Elizabeth, Anna, Alice, Lillian, Rose, Margaret) are reviving; the era-specific ones (Mildred, Helen, Dorothy) remain mostly in dormancy. The gap between these groups is widening.

**The great concentration.** In 1914, the top 15 boys' names accounted for an estimated 35% of all male births. In 2024, the top 15 boys' names account for around 18%. Naming in 1914 was dramatically more homogeneous — there were far fewer names in circulation, and the leading names dominated in a way modern parents find almost impossible to imagine.

**The vintage revival, measured.** Of the 1914 girls' list: Alice is at #57, Lillian at #77, Rose at #87, Margaret at #209, Florence at #364, Mabel at #285. These are the names parents in 2024 think of as "charming and old but not too old." Names from 1914 now have the century of distance that makes them feel genuinely fresh again.
