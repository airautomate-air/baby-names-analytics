The three most popular girls' names in 2024 are Olivia, Emma, and Amelia. Two of those are four syllables. The next three — Charlotte, Mia, Sophia — include Charlotte (3 syllables) and Sophia (3). Of the top 10 girls' names, only Evelyn (#8), Ava (#9), and Sofia (#10) are under 3 syllables.

American girls' naming has shifted toward longer, more elaborate names — while boys' naming remains dominated by short names. Here's what the data shows.

---

## The top four-syllable girls' names

### Olivia — #1 (14,718 babies)

**[Olivia](/name/olivia.html)** — Latin *oliva* (olive), with the -ia feminine suffix. O-LIV-i-a: four syllables. Olivia has been #1 or #2 since 2018. The four-syllable structure doesn't make it unwieldy — it flows naturally, with the stress on the second syllable. Short form Liv is clean and modern.

### Amelia — #3 (12,740 babies)

**[Amelia](/name/amelia.html)** — from Germanic *Amalia* (labor/work), Latinized. A-MEL-i-a: four syllables. Amelia Earhart, Amelia Bedelia. The stress pattern (second syllable) is the same as Olivia, which may explain why both feel equally natural despite their length.

### Isabella — #7 (10,770 babies)

**[Isabella](/name/isabella.html)** — Italian feminine of Isaiah. Is-a-BELL-a: four syllables. Isabella was #1 in 2009 and 2010 at the *Twilight* peak, when Bella Swan's full name drove interest. It has declined from that peak but is still #7. Short forms: Bella (#108), Belle (#1,006), Izzy.

### Valentina — #47 (4,438 babies)

**[Valentina](/name/valentina.html)** — Italian/Spanish feminine of Valentine. Val-en-TEE-na: four syllables. One of the most successfully elaborate names currently climbing. Short forms: Val, Tina (though Tina is currently considered retro in a negative way), Lena.

### Serenity — #117 (2,342 babies)

**[Serenity](/name/serenity.html)** — English, from Latin *serenitas* (clearness, peace). Se-REN-i-ty: four syllables. Serenity is the virtue/nature name that parents choose when they want something longer than Hope or Grace. See [virtue names](/blog/virtue-names.html).

### Cecilia — #123 (2,238 babies)

**[Cecilia](/name/cecilia.html)** — Latin, from *caecus* (blind); Saint Cecilia is the patron saint of music. Ce-CIL-i-a: four syllables. The Simon & Garfunkel song (1970) kept Cecilia visible through its mid-century fall. Now at #123 and climbing.

### Catalina — #128 (2,216 babies)

**[Catalina](/name/catalina.html)** — Spanish form of Catherine. Cat-a-LEE-na: four syllables. Catalina Island off the coast of Los Angeles has made this a recognizable place name in California; it also has the Italian/Spanish feminine elaborateness that's driving the current trend.

### Magnolia — #138 (2,146 babies)

**[Magnolia](/name/magnolia.html)** — the flower, named for Pierre Magnol (French botanist). Mag-NO-li-a: four syllables. Magnolia is the most exuberant of the botanical names currently in use. Chip and Joanna Gaines named their daughter Magnolia and built a home design empire around the name.

### Evangeline — #174 (1,741 babies)

**[Evangeline](/name/evangeline.html)** — from Greek *evangelos* (good news, gospel), with the Latin feminine -ine suffix. E-VAN-ge-line: four syllables. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's 1847 epic poem *Evangeline* established it as an American literary name. Now climbing — particularly appealing to parents who want something longer than Evan or Evelyn.

### Anastasia — #166 (1,832 babies)

**[Anastasia](/name/anastasia.html)** — Greek, from *anastasis* (resurrection). A-nas-TA-si-a: five syllables — the longest common name in current data. Princess Anastasia Romanov (the youngest daughter of Tsar Nicholas II, who died in 1918) has kept this name in public consciousness. The 1997 animated film helped. Short forms: Ana, Stacy, Nastia (Russian).

## Even longer: names with 5 syllables

**Anastasia** (#166) at five syllables is the most prominent, but there are others:

- **Evangelina** (variant of Evangeline): less common but present
- **Alexandria** (#464): al-ex-AN-dri-a = 5 syllables
- **Alexandrina**: extremely rare but elegant

---

## Long names for girls vs. short names for boys

The syllable pattern is strikingly asymmetric:

**Girls top-10:**
Olivia (4), Emma (2), Amelia (4), Charlotte (3), Mia (2), Sophia (3), Isabella (4), Evelyn (3), Ava (2), Sofia (3)

**Boys top-10:**
Liam (2), Noah (2), Oliver (3), James (1), Elijah (3), Mateo (3), Theodore (3), Henry (2), Lucas (2), Luca (2)

Girls' names average significantly more syllables than boys' names. This has been true in American naming for decades — English naming has long preferred shorter, punchier names for boys and longer, more elaborate names for girls. But the current gap is more pronounced than at any point in the SSA data.

---

## Why long names work now

**Nicknames provide flexibility.** Isabella → Bella or Izzy. Valentina → Val or Tina. Magnolia → Maggie or Nola. Anastasia → Ana or Stacy. Long names give the child options; the formal name is for official contexts, the nickname is for everyday.

**The elaborateness signals investment.** A four-syllable name requires more commitment than a two-syllable name. For parents who want to signal that the naming choice was deliberate and meaningful, longer names carry that signal.

**The Italian and Spanish -ia ending.** Most of the long girls' names end in -ia: Olivia, Amelia, Isabella, Catalina, Anastasia, Cecilia, Valeria, Aurelia. This ending is the classical Latin/Greek feminine suffix, and it naturally produces longer names. See [names ending in -a](/blog/names-ending-in-a.html).

**Romance languages in American naming.** The Italian/Spanish influence on American naming (driven by immigration patterns and by the aesthetic appeal of Romance-language sounds) systematically introduces longer names: Valentina, Antonella, Alessia, Esmeralda, Evangelina. These names have no English-language short form built in.

## Boys: Alexander and the rare exceptions

### Alexander — #27 (7,517 babies)

**[Alexander](/name/alexander.html)** — Greek *Alexandros*: *alexein* (to defend) + *aner* (man): "defender of men." Al-ex-AN-der: four syllables. Alexander the Great; Alexander Hamilton; Alexander McQueen. At #27, Alexander is the dominant four-syllable boys' name and has been in the top 30 since 1980.

Short forms: Alex (#205), Xander (#215), Alec (#419), Lex.

### Ezekiel — #54 (5,390 babies)

**[Ezekiel](/name/ezekiel.html)** — Hebrew, "God strengthens." E-ZEE-ki-el: four syllables. At #54. Short form: Zeke.

### Nathaniel — #144 (2,511 babies)

**[Nathaniel](/name/nathaniel.html)** — Hebrew, "God has given." Na-THAN-i-el: four syllables. Short form: Nate, Nathan (#62 as a standalone).

## Long names that are on the way

Several elaborate names are still rare but climbing fast:

**Aurelia** (#335, 933 babies) — Latin, "golden." Au-REEL-i-a: four syllables. More elaborate than Aurora but in the same family.

**Octavia** (#294, 1,066 babies) — Latin, "eighth." Oc-TAY-vi-a: four syllables. Roman imperial; *Downton Abbey* character; *Hunger Games* tribute. Climbing.

**Seraphina** (outside top 500) — from Hebrew *seraphim* (the burning ones, a class of angel). Se-ra-PHEE-na: four syllables. Ben Affleck and Jennifer Garner named their daughter Seraphina in 2009. Still rare but has the elaborate, angelic quality that's trending.

**Persephone** (#737, 254 babies) — Greek goddess of the underworld. Per-SEF-o-ne: four syllables. The most mythologically dramatic of the long names currently in use.
