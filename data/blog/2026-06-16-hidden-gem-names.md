The top 25 names are known by every parent in the waiting room. But in the 200–400 range, there are names that have all the qualities of the top names — good sounds, clear meanings, distinguished histories — without the ubiquity. Your child will meet one or two others with these names, not six.

Here are the hidden gems from 2024 SSA data.

---

## Girls: Hidden Gem Names

### Arabella — #206 (1,476 babies)

**[Arabella](/name/arabella.html)** is one of the longest and most elaborate names currently in use — five syllables, Italian origin (from Latin *orabilis*, easily entreated; or a medieval form of Amabel). It has the kind of name that takes up a whole first-grade introduction: "Hi, I'm Arabella." That's part of its appeal. *Doctor Who* has a character named Arabella; *Downton Abbey* associates it with Edwardian femininity. At #206, it's distinctive without being unusual.

### Wren — #213 (1,442 babies)

**[Wren](/name/wren.html)** — the small, fierce North American bird. One syllable, entirely complete, climbing fast (it was #400 as recently as 2018). Wren has the same quality as Eve, Dawn, and Fern: a single-syllable nature word that works perfectly as a name. It's an outlier in the current naming environment — almost all the top 200 names are two to four syllables.

### Dahlia — #240 (1,300 babies)

**[Dahlia](/name/dahlia.html)** — the flower, named for the Swedish botanist Anders Dahl. The *Black Dahlia* murder (1947) has cast a shadow over the name for decades, but that case is now 75+ years old and its cultural salience has faded. Dahlia as a flower name is climbing for the same reasons that Violet, Iris, and Poppy are: botanical names have a vintage quality that feels current.

### Maisie — #255 (1,241 babies)

**[Maisie](/name/maisie.html)** — Scottish diminutive of Margaret (from Gaelic *Mairead*). Maisie Williams (*Game of Thrones*' Arya Stark) has been the primary celebrity bearer since 2011. The name is warm, informal, and thoroughly Scottish — which gives it the same cross-Atlantic feel as Isla, Rory, and Callum.

### Ophelia — #261 (1,215 babies)

**[Ophelia](/name/ophelia.html)** — Shakespeare's most tragic female character (*Hamlet*). Until recently, Ophelia was the paradigm case of a name too weighted by literary association to be used easily. Now it's at #261 and climbing — parents are choosing it precisely because of the literary weight. Shakespeare's Ophelia is tragic but visually striking (the drowning scene; the Pre-Raphaelite paintings); the name has more cultural depth than most names in the top 500.

### Thea — #348 (893 babies)

**[Thea](/name/thea.html)** — Greek goddess name (Titaness of sight and light; mother of Helios the sun god). Also used as a short form of Theodora, Dorothea, or Anthea. Two syllables, completely clear, lovely sound. At #348, Thea is quieter than it deserves to be — it has the same clean elegance as Nora, Clara, and Vera.

### Esme — #345 (901 babies)

**[Esme](/name/esme.html)** — French/Persian (from *esmer*, esteemed, or from the Old French *esmé*, loved). Esme was introduced to American parents largely through *Twilight*'s Esme Cullen (Carlisle's wife). Unlike many *Twilight* names, Esme has sustained itself well — at #345 eleven years after the franchise ended, it's the name that benefited from the pop culture moment without being defined by it.

### Elodie — #371 (839 babies)

**[Elodie](/name/elodie.html)** — French form of Alodia (from Germanic *ala*, foreign, + *od*, wealth; related to melody). Elodie sounds like a word that should mean something musical — and the sound is striking: EH-lo-dee, three flowing syllables. It's common in France and is crossing to American use through the French-name trend (the same wave that brought Delphine, Margot, and Sylvie).

### Astrid — #383 (818 babies)

**[Astrid](/name/astrid.html)** — Old Norse (*áss*, god + *fríðr*, beautiful): divine beauty. Astrid is a Scandinavian royal name (Queen Astrid of Belgium; several Norwegian queens) and was used across the Viking world. It's distinctive in the American naming landscape — sounds Scandinavian and strong without being hard to say.

### Colette — #400 (789 babies)

**[Colette](/name/colette.html)** — French feminine form of Nicholas. Also the pen name of the French author Sidonie-Gabrielle Colette (1873–1954), who wrote *Gigi* and *Claudine*. The *Pixar* film *Ratatouille* (2007) features Colette as one of its main characters. Colette has a precise French-intellectual quality: sophisticated, slightly serious, distinctly itself.

## Boys: Hidden Gem Names

### Oscar — #217 (1,656 babies)

**[Oscar](/name/oscar.html)** — Old Norse (*Ásgeir*, god + spear) or Old Irish (*Osgar*, friend of deer). Oscar is the prestigious boys' name that feels like it should be in the top 50 but isn't. Oscar Wilde; *Schindler's List*'s Oscar Schindler; the Academy Award named Oscar; Oscar the Grouch. The name is top-5 in several European countries (Ireland, Spain, Sweden). The US is behind.

### Atticus — #277 (1,223 babies)

**[Atticus](/name/atticus.html)** — Roman cognomen (from *Attica*, the region of Greece); most famously Atticus Finch in *To Kill a Mockingbird* (1960). Atticus is the most specifically literary boys' name currently climbing. It was essentially unused before 2000 — #1,429 in 2000 — and is now #277. The *Go Set a Watchman* controversy (2015) briefly caused a dip when Harper Lee's Atticus was portrayed more problematically, but the name recovered. Now it's a name with philosophical and legal weight.

### Tobias — #280 (1,210 babies)

**[Tobias](/name/tobias.html)** — Hebrew (*Toviyah*, God is good). Tobias is the full form of Toby, and at #280 it's at a similar level to where Theodore was before its revival. The German and Dutch tradition (Tobias is common in Germany; Tobias Fünke on *Arrested Development*) has kept it present. It has exactly the kind of multi-syllable vintage-revival quality that Theodore, Sebastian, and Atticus share.

### Orion — #323 (1,061 babies)

**[Orion](/name/orion.html)** — the hunter constellation; Greek mythological hero. Orion has been climbing steadily with the mythology and astronomy naming trend. It's more distinctive than Atlas (#101) and more recognizable than Rigel or Altair. Three syllables, strong vowels, clear meaning.

### Rhys — #353 (946 babies)

**[Rhys](/name/rhys.html)** — Welsh, from *rhy*, ardor or enthusiasm (related to the Old Welsh *rhethu*, to rush). Rhys is the clearest example of a Welsh name crossing into American mainstream use. Jonathan Rhys Meyers; Matt Rhys (*The Americans*). The pronunciation (REES) is non-obvious from the spelling, which may limit some parents; but for those who know it, it's a name with significant distinction.

### Sullivan — #339 (1,011 babies)

**[Sullivan](/name/sullivan.html)** — Irish (*Ó Súileabháin*, dark-eyed). Sullivan has been climbing since around 2010. It has a natural short form (Sully) that feels warm and contemporary. Sully from *Monsters, Inc.* (2001) has probably helped. At #339, Sullivan is genuinely underused given its qualities.

### Desmond — #368 (888 babies)

**[Desmond](/name/desmond.html)** — Irish (*Deasmhumhain*, of South Munster; a place name that became a family name). Desmond Tutu; Desmond Hume from *Lost* (one of television's most beloved characters, 2004–2010). Desmond has an Irish/African historical weight and a warm -nd ending that places it in the same family as Edmund and Raymond.

### Wilder — #390 (830 babies)

**[Wilder](/name/wilder.html)** — Old English (*wildēor*, wild animal) or the surname of a family "from the wild." Billy Wilder (the director); Gene Wilder (Willy Wonka). Wilder is the frontier-nature name that's arriving as the successor to Hunter and Archer — it's climbing faster than either, and still below #400.

### Wells — #375 (865 babies)

**[Wells](/name/wells.html)** — English place name/occupational name (a person who lives near a well). H.G. Wells. Wells is the Wells Fargo banks as an American surname made familiar; now it's being used as a given name. Short, strong, slightly unusual — the place-name adjacent to Hayes and Brooks.

## What makes a hidden gem?

The names on this list share qualities:
- A clear pronunciation (even if the spelling is unusual, like Rhys)
- A defined cultural reference or meaning that parents can explain
- A distinctly individual sound without being invented
- Current upward momentum — these are names climbing toward mainstream, not declining toward obscurity

The sweet spot for a truly distinctive name is approximately rank #200–600: present enough to feel like a name rather than a word, absent enough that your child won't share a classroom with four of them.
