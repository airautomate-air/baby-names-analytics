We wrote earlier about [French names that haven't crossed the Atlantic yet](/blog/french-names-not-in-us.html) — names like Sacha, Ambre, and Victoire that are big in France but invisible in the US. The flip side is just as interesting: the names that work fluently in both countries simultaneously. No translation required, no pronunciation confusion, no cultural barrier. These names are in the top 200 in France and the top 200 in the US at the same time.

Here are sixteen of them.

## Boys

**[Noah](/name/noah.html)** — France's #1 boy name for most of the 2010s. US #5 in 2024. Noah has been the dominant international boys' name of this generation — it requires no adjustment crossing any border because it comes from a shared religious tradition both cultures recognize.

**[Liam](/name/liam.html)** — US #2. Also inside France's top 15. Originally Irish, Liam has transcended its origin and become genuinely global — without an accent, easy to pronounce in both French and English, short enough to survive any context.

**[Lucas](/name/lucas.html)** — US #10. France top 20 for years. Lucas is one of the few names that managed to dominate the French charts (it was #1 in France for several years) and simultaneously hold the US top 15. The Latin form works in every Romance and Germanic language.

**[Leo](/name/leo.html)** — US #4. France top 30. Two syllables in French ("lay-OH"), one in English. Both pronunciations are valid; neither country feels the name is foreign. Pope Leo XIII and Leo Messi give it anchoring in two very different cultural registers.

**[Louis](/name/louis.html)** — US #79. France top 10 consistently. Americans pronounce it "LOO-iss"; the French say "lwee." Both versions are used in both countries without friction. Fourteen French kings help on the French side; Louis Armstrong and Louis Vuitton bridge the Atlantic.

**[Gabriel](/name/gabriel.html)** — US #16. France top 25. The archangel's name is among the most internationally portable names in existence. It crosses into Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, German, and Swedish with identical spelling; France and the US are just two more of its homes.

**[Adam](/name/adam.html)** — US #71. France top 40. Another name whose biblical origin makes it feel native in any Western culture. Short, easy, no gendered ambiguity, no spelling variants to fight about.

**[Theo](/name/theo.html)** — US #13 (as standalone; Theodore is #11). France top 10. Theo has been one of the fastest-rising boys' names in Europe this decade, and it's achieved the same in the US. The short form skips the theodore/théodore spelling divergence entirely.

## Girls

**[Emma](/name/emma.html)** — US #2. France #1 for most of the 2010s. Emma is the single most universally portable girls' name of the current generation. German origin, universally adopted, requires nothing from the speaker in any language.

**[Jade](/name/jade.html)** — US #116. France top 10. Jade has been a perennial top-10 girls' name in France for two decades. In the US it's more mid-chart but solidly present. The gemstone naming trend appears to cut across both naming cultures.

**[Alice](/name/alice.html)** — US #57. France top 20. Lewis Carroll's Alice is recognizable everywhere, and the name itself is the same in French and English — same spelling, similar enough pronunciation. Alice has been reviving strongly in both countries simultaneously since about 2015.

**[Charlotte](/name/charlotte.html)** — US #3. France top 20. Royally associated in both countries (Princess Charlotte in Britain, multiple French queens). Charlotte has been the gold standard of Atlantic-crossing girls' names for two decades.

**[Camille](/name/camille.html)** — US #160. France top 5 girls for years. In France, Camille is solidly feminine. In the US, where it's less familiar, it's used nearly exclusively for girls too. It crosses without translation because the spelling is identical; Americans just have to learn to say "ka-MEE" instead of "ka-MILL."

**[Juliette](/name/juliette.html)** — US #136. France top 20. The extra '-tte' at the end of Juliet gives it a French feel even in English-speaking countries, and it sits comfortably in the top 200 on both sides of the Atlantic.

**[Nora](/name/nora.html)** — US #27. France top 30. Irish and Arabic in origin (Nora/Noura), Nora has achieved bilingual status by being phonetically simple and requiring no adjustment from either language community.

**[Clara](/name/clara.html)** — US #109. France top 25. Latin origin, identical spelling in both languages, equally familiar from Saint Clara (Sainte-Claire) in both Catholic traditions. Clara has been quietly consistent in both charts for decades.

## What makes a name bilingual?

Looking at these sixteen names, three patterns emerge:

**1. Latin or biblical origin covers a lot.** Noah, Gabriel, Adam, Lucas, Leo, Louis, Clara — names rooted in Latin or Hebrew scripture feel native in any Western Christian culture. Neither country can claim ownership; both can claim tradition.

**2. Short names travel better.** Liam, Jade, Nora, Leo, Adam. Two syllables maximum, no tricky clusters. The longer names that made the list (Charlotte, Juliette, Camille) all have the same spelling in both languages, which removes friction.

**3. Celebrity and culture help but aren't required.** Emma got a boost from Emma Watson. Charlotte from royals on both sides of the Channel. But Noah and Alice made it without a single dominant modern bearer — the underlying name was simply portable enough on its own.
