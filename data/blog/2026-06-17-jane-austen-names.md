Jane Austen published six novels between 1811 and 1817. They've never gone out of print. And their characters' names have become some of the most influential in American baby naming — not just because of the books, but because of the adaptations that have kept them alive and aspirational for 200 years.

The irony is that Austen herself was named after a saint, not a character. But her heroines — Emma, Charlotte, Elizabeth, Anne, Marianne, Elinor, Catherine — have shaped naming culture in ways she couldn't have imagined.

## Emma: from Austen heroine to #1

**[Emma](/name/emma.html)** — Girls #8, 14,621 babies in 2024.

Emma Woodhouse — "handsome, clever, and rich, with a comfortable home and happy disposition" — is Austen's most self-satisfied heroine. She's also given her name to more American girls than any other literary character in history.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #17 | 9,204 |
| 2000 | #12 | 11,320 |
| 2004 | #3 | 14,891 |
| 2008 | #2 | 18,587 |
| 2012 | **#1** | **20,491** |
| 2014 | #1 | 20,799 |
| 2018 | #2 | 18,688 |
| 2022 | #4 | 15,340 |
| 2024 | #8 | 14,621 |

Emma was #1 for five consecutive years (2014-2018). It's now declining from that peak, which is the natural cycle for any name that dominant (see [generational-names](/blog/generational-names.html)). The name benefited from: the 1996 film with Gwyneth Paltrow, the 2009 BBC miniseries, the 2020 film adaptation, and the general Jane Austen renaissance that has run continuously since the 1990s.

Emma is also a short, clean, easy-to-spell name that works across languages — an advantage that compounds all the literary associations.

## Charlotte: Austen's most popular name in 2024

**[Charlotte](/name/charlotte.html)** — Girls #4, 16,311 babies in 2024.

Charlotte Lucas is a secondary character in *Pride and Prejudice* — Elizabeth Bennet's practical friend who makes the sensible-but-disappointing choice to marry Mr. Collins. Yet her name has outranked Elizabeth in American naming for a decade.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #110 | 2,698 |
| 2000 | #110 | 3,041 |
| 2004 | #83 | 4,694 |
| 2010 | #45 | 7,236 |
| 2014 | #12 | 14,080 |
| 2018 | **#6** | **15,878** |
| 2020 | #3 | 16,462 |
| 2024 | **#4** | **16,311** |

Charlotte got an enormous boost from two Royal Baby moments: Charlotte, Princess of Wales was born in 2015 (see [royal-baby-names](/blog/royal-baby-names.html)), and the name was already top-20 when it happened. The royal naming accelerated what was already a strong trend. Charlotte is now so established in the top 5 that it reads as a classic rather than a trend name.

## Elizabeth: the name that held on

**[Elizabeth](/name/elizabeth.html)** — Girls #20, 12,042 babies in 2024.

Elizabeth Bennet is the most beloved of all Austen heroines — witty, principled, and independent. The name Elizabeth has been in continuous top-25 use for over a century, making it one of the most durable names in American history.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1954 | **#5** | 25,892 |
| 1974 | #13 | 14,480 |
| 1994 | #4 | 21,942 |
| 2004 | #9 | 16,082 |
| 2014 | #13 | 12,912 |
| 2020 | #17 | 10,962 |
| 2024 | #20 | 12,042 |

Elizabeth's slight recovery in 2024 (from #17 to #20, up in raw count) suggests it may have bottomed out. It's one of the few names that can plausibly claim both timelessness AND current fashion — it's been in the top 20 for so long that it's essentially non-generational.

Queen Elizabeth II (who died in 2022) gave the name a final sentimental boost in the years after her passing, visible in a slight uptick in 2023-2024.

## Eleanor: the breakout Austen name

**[Eleanor](/name/eleanor.html)** — Girls #35, 7,901 babies in 2024.

Elinor Dashwood is the composed, rational elder sister in *Sense and Sensibility*. The modern spelling Eleanor (the traditional Plantagenet form) has had the most dramatic rise of any Austen name.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 1994 | #230 | 1,275 |
| 2004 | #372 | 878 |
| 2010 | #207 | 1,666 |
| 2014 | #93 | 4,427 |
| 2018 | #51 | 6,770 |
| 2022 | **#33** | **8,152** |
| 2024 | #35 | 7,901 |

Eleanor was already part of the vintage revival before *Sense and Sensibility* adaptations, but the association helped. The name benefits from the Roosevelt connection (Eleanor Roosevelt), the nickname Ellie (which is independently top-30), and the melodic four-syllable flow that parents seeking an "elaborate" name find satisfying. See [long-baby-names](/blog/long-baby-names.html).

## Darcy: the heroine tribute that took decades

**[Darcy](/name/darcy.html)** — Girls #549, 549 babies in 2024.

Fitzwilliam Darcy is one of the most famous male literary characters in the English language. His first name is almost never used. But the surname Darcy — borrowed and reassigned as a girls' name — has been climbing quietly for years.

| Year | Rank (girls) | Babies |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 2000 | ~#800 | ~260 |
| 2010 | ~#700 | ~340 |
| 2014 | ~#750 | ~310 |
| 2020 | #596 | 494 |
| 2024 | **#549** | **549** |

Darcy as a girls name is a tribute name for *Pride and Prejudice* fans — the choice of parents who are naming their daughter after the romantic ideal, not the character himself. It's a different mechanism than most Austen name effects: instead of naming after a heroine, parents are naming after the love interest, gender-flipping the surname.

## What Austen did to naming

Jane Austen's influence on American baby naming is both direct (Emma, Charlotte, Elizabeth) and indirect (the general revival of Regency-era elegance that also lifts Bridgerton names, see [bridgerton-names](/blog/bridgerton-names.html)).

The key mechanism: Austen adaptations keep arriving, introducing each new generation of parents to her heroines. The 1995 BBC *Pride and Prejudice* created Colin Firth, the 1996 *Emma* created Gwyneth Paltrow's career peak, the 2005 Keira Knightley film introduced millennials. Now Gen Z parents who grew up with those films are naming their daughters.

The pattern suggests Austen will keep influencing naming as long as her work remains in print — which shows no sign of stopping.
