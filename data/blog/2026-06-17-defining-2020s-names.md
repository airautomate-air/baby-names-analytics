Every decade in SSA data has a distinct character — a set of names and patterns that define it and separate it from what came before and what comes after.

The 1950s: Mary, James, Robert, Patricia.
The 1970s: Jennifer, Michael, Jason, Heather.
The 1990s: Jessica, Ashley, Christopher, Tyler.
The 2010s: Emma, Olivia, Liam, Noah, Sophia.
The 2020s: something new is forming.

We're halfway through the decade. The SSA data through 2024 is clear enough to identify what's different — and to make educated predictions about what the 2020s cohort will be called when they're adults.

---

## The 2020s signature: more diversity at the top

The most fundamental shift in the 2020s is the fragmentation of the top — no single name dominates the way Jennifer did in the 1970s (25,000+ babies per year at peak) or Michael did in the 1960s (80,000+ babies per year at peak).

| Year | #1 Girls Name | Babies | #1 Boys Name | Babies |
|------|--------------|--------|-------------|--------|
| 1960 | Mary | 47,292 | David | 85,928 |
| 1980 | Jennifer | 58,385 | Michael | 68,704 |
| 2000 | Emily | 25,954 | Jacob | 34,465 |
| 2010 | Isabella | 22,925 | Jacob | 22,924 |
| 2020 | Olivia | 17,535 | Liam | 19,659 |
| 2024 | Olivia | 16,573 | Liam | 20,456 |

The #1 name in 2024 — Olivia — has 16,573 babies. The #1 name in 1960 — Mary — had 47,292. America's baby naming has fragmented as the population diversified and regional/cultural pockets retained their own naming traditions rather than converging on national trends.

This fragmentation means the 2020s are defined less by specific names than by categories and aesthetics.

## The 2020s defining trends

### 1. Latin-origin names go mainstream

The single biggest story. Mateo (#5 boys), Luna (#10 girls), Santiago (#35 boys), Valentina (#71 girls), Camila (#28 girls), Sofia (#18 girls). Names with Spanish or Latin roots are now competing in the overall top 50 at rates unprecedented in American naming history.

This reflects demographic reality — the Hispanic share of the US population crossed 19% — and cultural prestige. See [latin-american-names-us](/blog/latin-american-names-us.html).

### 2. The celestial and mythological wave

Aurora (#36), Luna (#10), Freya (#22), Orion (#159), Odin (#365), Phoenix (#326). Names that evoke stars, mythology, and the natural cosmos are more mainstream than at any point in modern American naming. The pandemic accelerated this trend in 2020-2022 (see [pandemic-baby-names](/blog/pandemic-baby-names.html)).

### 3. Vintage revival deepening

Names that were last popular in the 1910s-1940s are now fully mainstream: Hazel (#37), Violet (#97), Theodore (#16), Walter (#241, still rising), Eleanor (#35), Edith (entering the top 500). The [skip-generation](/blog/skip-generation-names.html) pattern is operating at full speed.

### 4. Old Testament anchor names

Levi (#15), Isaiah (#36), Ezekiel (#66), Elijah (#9), Ezra (#24). Hebrew patriarch and prophet names are the new traditionalism — chosen by religious families and secular parents who want names with roots and meaning. See [old-testament-names](/blog/old-testament-names.html).

### 5. The -son and -ley waves peak

Jackson (#24), Mason (#23), Grayson (#40) for boys; Riley (#36), Everly (#72), Paisley (#73), Kinsley (#95) for girls — these are the defining patterns of the late 2010s/early 2020s. See [son-ending-names](/blog/son-ending-names.html) and [ley-ending-names](/blog/ley-ending-names.html).

## Names that will define this decade's cohort

When the babies born in 2020-2029 are in kindergarten in the 2030s, what will the classrooms look like?

**Every class will have a:**
- Olivia or Emma (inevitable, they're been top-5 for 15 years)
- Liam or Noah (same for boys)
- Luna or Aurora (the celestial wave)
- Elijah or Levi (the biblical wave)

**Most classes will have:**
- A Mateo and a Santiago (the Latin crossover)
- A Freya or Astrid (the Norse wave)
- A Theodore or Oliver (the vintage revival)
- An Everly or Kinsley (the -ley wave)

**The distinctive names:**
- Wren, Briar, Fern, Sage — the botanical minimalists
- Maverick, Archer, Knox — the bold masculine statement names
- Seraphina, Evangeline, Celestine — the grand feminine names
- Zephyr, Orion, Atlas — the celestial adventure names

## What the 2020s are NOT doing

**Declining in the 2020s:**
- Traditional top-20 staples like Madison, Addison, Ashley, Jessica
- Peak-1990s names: Taylor, Tyler, Brittany, Megan
- Classic surname-names that peaked in the 2010s: Hunter, Chase, Tanner
- The Jayden/Brayden/Zayden family (declining from peak)

**Never recovered:**
- Names associated with specific celebrities who faded: Britney, Hilary, Lindsay
- Names with strong 1980s associations: Tiffany, Heather, Todd, Chad
- Names from political figures who divided: Monica, Hillary (both declined post-scandal)

## The 2020s name aesthetic

If the 2010s naming aesthetic was "surname-as-first-name" (Mason, Jackson, Harper, Piper), the 2020s aesthetic might be called **"roots and meaning"**.

Parents in the 2020s seem to be seeking names that:
- Have ancient etymology (Old Testament, classical, mythology)
- Connect to the natural world (celestial, botanical, earth-based)
- Cross linguistic and cultural boundaries (Latin, Norse, Italian)
- Sound complete rather than elaborate (Levi rather than Levi-Nathan; Wren rather than Wrenna)

The decade is still forming. By 2029 or 2030, when we have the full data, the 2020s character will be clearer. But the direction is already visible: this is the decade that said goodbye to the surname-name wave and said hello to the ancient world — celestial, mythological, biblical, and botanical.
