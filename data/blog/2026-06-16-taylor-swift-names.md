Taylor Swift is arguably the most famous person in the world right now. She has sold more concert tickets than any artist in history, had multiple #1 albums in a row, and has been credited with influencing everything from the NFL's ratings to the stock market.

And the name Taylor has been declining for two decades.

---

## Taylor — the paradox

**[Taylor](/name/taylor.html)** was #10 for girls in 2000 — when Taylor Swift was 10 years old and hadn't released any music. The name peaked in the mid-1990s when Taylor was a rising surname-first-name trend, driven by the same cultural force as Madison, Carter, and Hunter:

| Year | Girls Rank | Babies |
|------|-----------|--------|
| 1994 | **#3** | ~28,000 |
| 2000 | #10 | 15,080 |
| 2005 | #24 | 8,676 |
| 2010 | #36 | 5,900 |
| 2015 | #77 | 3,754 |
| 2020 | #162 | 1,747 |
| 2024 | **#353** | **877** |

Taylor peaked *before* Swift became famous. By the time she released *Fearless* (2008) and became a cultural phenomenon, Taylor was already declining from its 1990s peak as a trendy surname-first-name choice.

The irony: Taylor Swift may be the reason the name isn't falling *faster*. Without her, Taylor might be in the same trajectory as Ashley or Brittany — names that were #1 in the 1990s and are now essentially abandoned. Swift's permanent cultural presence has kept the name from completely collapsing, but it hasn't reversed the decline.

See [generational names](/blog/generational-names.html) and [surname names](/blog/surname-names.html) for the context.

## Why pop stars can't save their own names

The pattern across musicians is consistent: fame doesn't help your name if that name is already associated with a different generation.

**Adele**: The British singer-songwriter went from unknown to one of the best-selling artists ever with *21* (2011). The name Adele bumped from #910 (2010) to #536 (2012) — a real but modest effect — then settled around #800 in 2024. Adele is a French/German name (*Adelheid*, noble) that was declining from its mid-century peak; Adele's fame didn't reverse the trend, just created a floor.

**Rihanna**: Multiple Billboard #1s, one of the best-selling artists of all time. The name Rihanna went from #587 in 2010 to #1,756 in 2024 — *declining* throughout her career peak. Why? The name reads too directly as "named after Rihanna," which makes it feel like a celebrity tribute rather than a given name.

**Drake**: One of the best-selling musicians of all time; dominated hip-hop through the 2010s. Drake (the name) went from #196 in 2010 to #661 in 2024. The name Drake was a late-1990s surname trend that had already peaked.

**Travis**: Travis Scott is one of the most successful rappers of the past decade. The name Travis went from #178 in 2010 to #361 in 2024 — continuing a long decline. Travis is a classic 1970s-1990s American name that reads as a middle-aged man's name.

**Bruno**: Bruno Mars won seven Grammy Awards and had massive crossover success. Bruno went from #756 (2010) to #701 (2024) — essentially unchanged, though the *Encanto* effect (2021, Bruno from *We Don't Talk About Bruno*) is a confounding factor. See [Disney names](/blog/disney-names.html).

## The Billie Eilish exception

**[Billie](/name/billie.html)** — Girls #694 (405 babies). Billie Eilish burst onto the pop scene in 2019 (*When We All Fall Asleep, Where Do We Go?*) as a teenager, becoming one of the most distinctive voices of her generation.

| Year | Rank | Babies |
|------|------|--------|
| 2015 | #2,016 | 98 |
| 2019 | #1,361 | 155 |
| 2020 | #1,009 | 251 |
| 2022 | #706 | 412 |
| 2024 | **#694** | **405** |

Billie's climb from 2019 onward correlates with Eilish's breakout — and the timing suggests a real effect. But Billie is also a vintage name (Billie Holiday, the legendary jazz singer) that was already on a vintage-revival trajectory. The Eilish effect and the vintage revival are both contributing.

Billie is distinct from Taylor and Adele because the vintage revival was already making it feel "fresh" — it's not a 1990s suburban-American name, it's a 1920s-1930s jazz-era name. Eilish gave the name contemporary cool at exactly the moment when parents were discovering it through the vintage revival. See [nickname names](/blog/nickname-names.html).

## What pop music can do

The data shows that musicians affect naming only in specific circumstances:

**Names with independent appeal:** Billie succeeds because it's a vintage name being rediscovered *and* a contemporary celebrity's name simultaneously. The chart careers wouldn't be enough alone; the vintage appeal wouldn't be enough alone.

**Indirect effects through songs:** Taylor Swift's *Folklore* album (2020) featured a character named **Betty** and a character named **James** (a woman). Betty went from #1,131 to #1,008 between 2018 and 2024 — a very modest uptick that might be Swift-related, but Betty was already reviving as a vintage name.

**New sonic/cultural aesthetics:** Beyoncé's impact on naming has been through style (the Ivy, Blue aesthetic from her daughter's name) and through cultural power rather than her own first name. See [flower names](/blog/flower-names.html) for Ivy's climb.

**Artists who become cultural touchstones:** The rule is similar to athletes — the largest effects come from artists who dominate for *decades*, not years. Michael Jackson had a measurable effect on boys' names (various Jackson variants); Elvis had an effect. But even those effects are smaller than one might expect.

## The Swiftie naming question

Parents who are deeply invested in Taylor Swift's music (Swifties) do appear to name children with Swift in mind — but not by naming them Taylor. Instead:

**Album/era aesthetic names:** Parents influenced by *Fearless*-era Swift might choose names like Brave or Fearless (neither is in standard naming data). *Red* inspired Scarlett associations; *1989* was about a time period. These are aesthetic influences, not direct naming effects.

**Character names from songs:** Swift's songs include characters named Betty, James, Dorothea, Marjorie, Carolina. Some of these may have small spillover effects, but the data doesn't show dramatic shifts.

The pattern: Swiftie naming influence is diffuse and aesthetic rather than direct. A Swift fan might be more likely to choose a vintage floral name (like Rosemary or Violet) because those *feel* like Swift names without being directly named for characters or the artist herself.

---

## The universal rule of celebrity naming

Pop stars, like athletes, like actors, have a consistent effect on naming: they can *amplify* a name that's already on its way up, but they cannot *create* a naming trend from nothing or reverse a name that's on its way down.

Taylor Swift has not saved Taylor. Rihanna has not saved Rihanna. Drake has not saved Drake.

Billie Eilish has amplified Billie — because the vintage revival was already lifting the name and Eilish provided contemporary visibility at exactly the right moment.

The only naming effects that seem to work against the generational decay are when a celebrity is so singular that they completely own the name in the cultural imagination for decades: Elvis, Marilyn, Beyoncé, Michael Jordan. And even those effects are moderate compared to the demographic weight of generational saturation.
